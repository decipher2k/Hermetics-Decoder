Hall opens this chapter by insisting that the true history and mission of Jesus have never been plainly preserved in either canonical or apocryphal sources. What survives is a mixture of fragment, theology, legend, polemic, and initiatory disguise. He therefore approaches Christianity comparatively and esoterically, not devotionally. Contradictions among early fathers, divergent chronologies, apocryphal extravagances, and pagan analogies all suggest to him that the public story has been layered over a more secret doctrine. The task is not to mock Christianity, but to recover the mystery it once carried.

He begins with the instability of the historical record itself. Irenaeus, writing in the second century and appealing to apostolic testimony, speaks as though Jesus lived into a more advanced age and taught longer than the familiar one-year or three-year ministry formulas would allow. Justin Martyr openly tells pagans that Christians proclaim nothing essentially alien to what they already believe of the sons of Jupiter. Hall uses such evidence to show that early Christianity was much closer to other mystery religions, and far less historically fixed, than later orthodoxy admitted. The number thirty-three, the crucifixion pattern, and the whole arc of the Gospel life may therefore belong not only to history but to an already established initiatory drama.

That possibility opens the door to Hall's larger distinction between Jesus and the Christos. The Christos is the universal divine principle, the saving and mediating power latent within man, known under many names in earlier traditions. Atys, Adonis, Bacchus, Orpheus, and similar figures represent this same solar and regenerative mystery in different symbolic dress. Jesus, on this reading, was an illumined teacher who preached and embodied the Christos, but later tradition fused the historical instructor with the cosmic principle until the two became difficult to separate. Salvation then ceased to mean awakening the indwelling divine man and came instead to mean adherence to an externalized narrative.

Hall strengthens this reading by pointing to the order of Melchizedek, comparative Eucharistic symbolism, the Bacchic resonances of names and monograms, and the pagan background of the dying and rising god. The title "after the order of Melchizedek" implies a priestly and initiatory succession rather than a unique exception standing outside all precedent. The bread and wine rite has clear antecedents. The monogram IHS and other Christian symbols can be linked to earlier cults. For Hall this does not weaken Christianity; it locates it within the perennial wisdom tradition and restores its symbolic dignity.

The Essenes then emerge as the likely custodians of the immediate matrix in which Jesus was formed. Hall presents them as ascetic, disciplined, communal, initiatory, Qabbalistic, and devoted to healing mind, soul, and body. They lived apart from ordinary commerce, practiced probation and gradation, and sought the coming of a messianic fulfillment. Joseph and Mary are treated as members or near associates of this order, and the apocryphal material about Mary's dedication and Joseph's selection is reinterpreted not as sentimental miracle but as evidence of a sacred preparation for the birth of a teacher. "Immaculate" here means purified and consecrated, not a crude suspension of natural law.

In Hall's reconstruction Jesus was reared, educated, and later initiated among such custodians of mystery wisdom, then traveled eastward to complete his preparation in the broader current of ancient doctrine. Having attained the Christening, or reunion with his own spiritual source, he returned as a revealer of the divine mind within man. His public teaching employed parables for the many and deeper allegories for the few. The lower world, he taught, is ruled by a formative power, and the Eternal Father sends into it the Divine Mind as mediator. This mind is broken, distributed, eaten at a sacred supper, and awakened within rational beings so that through it they may return to their source. The disciples then declare of the teacher that he himself is this Mind personified.

Hall next extends the secret Christian current into the legends of Joseph of Arimathea, the Holy Grail, Arthur, Merlin, and the Round Table. Joseph becomes custodian of the cup and spear, founder of a hidden succession, and bearer of the mystery to Britain. The Grail is linked not merely to Christian piety but to older cauldron, cup, and abundance symbolism from many traditions. Merlin is interpreted as an initiate of the serpent wisdom, Arthur as solar and initiatory king, the Round Table as zodiacal and cosmic order, and the Siege Perilous as the throne of perfected man. In this way the Christian mystery is shown migrating into medieval legend without losing its initiatory structure.

The chapter's overall conclusion is that mystic Christianity originally taught an inward crucifixion, resurrection, and ascension. The Christos is the divine mediator in every person; Jesus is the great revealer and exemplar of that fact; the Gospel life is part history and part ritual map; and later church dogma, miracle legends, and literalist piety buried much of this under devotional and institutional overlays. To recover Christianity's deepest meaning, Hall says, one must read it once more as a mystery tradition continuous with the ancient world and directed toward the awakening of the eternal self within man.
