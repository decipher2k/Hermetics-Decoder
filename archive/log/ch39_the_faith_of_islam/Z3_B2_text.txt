Hall begins by placing Christian hostility to Islam on display through old polemics against Mohammed and the Koran, only to show how shallow those attacks are. The Prophet's denial that God begets in a human fashion is not blasphemy but a consequence of a more abstract and commanding monotheism. From the outset Hall's purpose is to rescue Islam from caricature and to understand it as a genuine vehicle of sacred knowledge, discipline, and civilizational power. He does not flatten differences between religions, but he refuses to let inherited prejudice define Mohammed or the faith he founded.

The narrative of Mohammed's life is then retold in symbolic and humane terms. Born amid legends of prodigy, instructed through travel, exposed to Nestorian Christianity and Arabian tradition, and stabilized by the practical devotion of Khadijah, Mohammed appears as a man of inward struggle rather than a political schemer. His retreat to Mount Hira, his cry for a restored religion of Adam, the visitation of Gabriel, and the repeated bodily distress accompanying revelation all serve, for Hall, to locate the source of Islam in visionary urgency. Whatever one makes of the phenomena psychologically, the moral seriousness and sincerity of the man are central.

Hall is careful to defend Mohammed's character. The Prophet's simplicity of life, frugality, domestic humility, hatred of lying, refusal to exploit sacred funds, kindness to servants, devotion to prayer, and acceptance of suffering all contradict the stock image of a sensual impostor. Khadijah's faith, Abu Bekr's loyalty, A'isha's testimony, and the acknowledgment even of non-Muslim writers support the picture of a leader whose authority rested not only on force or policy but on recognized integrity. His migration, triumphal return to Mecca, destruction of idols in the Caaba, final pilgrimage, and death in Medina complete the arc of a religious founder rather than a mere conqueror.

The Caaba itself becomes one of the chapter's major symbols. Hall describes it as the cube-shaped sacred center of Islam, with the black stone embedded in its wall, the annual drapery, the holy precinct, the circumambulations, and the inherited sanctity of the site. All of this suggests to him that Islam absorbed and rededicated a much older center of mystery worship. The cube, the stone, the rites of circling, the association with celestial models, and the black relic all point beyond mere historical accident. Islam did not arise in a vacuum; it inherited, purified, and redirected older sacred forms.

On questions of doctrine and social ethics Hall again resists common prejudice. He disputes the claim that Islam denied woman a soul or made her salvation dependent upon a husband, and cites Qur'anic language placing men and women alike under the law of divine recompense. The Prophet's own words to his daughter and aunt underscore individual responsibility before God. Likewise, Hall treats the contradictions of the Koran not simply as proof of confusion but as the result of compilation, interpolation, and later political reshaping after Mohammed's death. What is noble, monotheistic, disciplined, and inwardly powerful in the text he tends to attribute to the Prophet's actual vision.

The chapter then widens into esoteric speculation. Hall suggests several possible channels through which Mohammed may have contacted the ancient wisdom tradition: direct inner schooling, Nestorian intermediaries, mysterious holy men, or surviving Arabian schools already rooted in old mystery cults. He also notices symbolic features within Islam itself, including the sanctity of Friday, the crescent, the feminine and generative associations of some of its inherited emblems, and the continued presence of pre-Islamic ceremonial forms transformed into monotheistic devotion. In this way Islam is integrated into the larger current of perennial religion without ceasing to be itself.

Finally Hall stresses Islam's civilizational greatness. While Christian Europe passed through darker centuries, the Islamic world cultivated philosophy, science, medicine, mathematics, optics, geography, and learning on a high level. Spain under Muslim rule becomes his leading example of refinement and scholarly brilliance, but he also names the great scientists and philosophers whose work shaped the later West. Islam is therefore not a peripheral or merely antagonistic religion; it is one of the great repositories through which ancient wisdom, disciplined monotheism, and scientific culture were preserved and transmitted.

The chapter closes by hinting at the cyclic reappearance of wisdom in figures like Pythagoras, Christ, Mohammed, and later world-shaping leaders. Whether or not one accepts the full speculative pattern, Hall's intent is clear: Islam belongs within the sacred history of humanity. Mohammed is to be read not as a crude impostor but as a reforming emissary of divine unity whose faith preserved both spiritual and intellectual treasures the modern world can ignore only at its own cost.
