Extraction

Central statements
- The chapter emphasizes that later ages inherit effects more readily than causes.
- Ruins and reports transmit pressure, but not always the key to operation.
- Interpretation must move from spectacle back toward principle.

Operative qualities
Aftereffect, inheritance, backward inference, principial reconstruction, and disciplined reading.

Reformulation

What later ages inherit from antiquity is often effect without method. A lamp is found still burning. A story persists of a tree or vase that spoke. A colossal monument survives in fragments. The immediate impression is powerful, but the generative principle has receded.

Hall's method is to move backward from aftereffect toward cause. He asks what worldview, discipline, and symbolic science would make such survivals intelligible. This is the opposite of merely enjoying spectacle.

The chapter therefore trains the reader in principial reconstruction. One must infer hidden order from remnants, not because certainty is always available, but because the remnant itself points beyond accident.

In this sense, wonder is a summons to deeper reading. The marvel demands that the observer recover a lost relation between form and meaning.
