Extraction

Central statements
- The chapter resolves into a doctrine of matter as archive of sacred process.
- Durable forms preserve not only symbols but modes of relation.
- Antiquity is presented as able to externalize inner science without exhausting it.

Operative qualities
Archival matter, durable relation, externalized inner science, and concealed continuity.

Reformulation

On the third pass, the chapter's deepest unity appears in its treatment of matter as archive. Lamps, groves, caves, tripods, obelisks, statues, towers, pyramids, and gardens are not simply symbolic surfaces. They preserve operative relations between force, form, and consciousness.

This is why Hall cares so much about survivals. Even when the doctrine once attached to them has dimmed, the forms still carry continuity. They hold enough of the original arrangement to provoke wonder and invite reconstruction.

The chapter therefore advances a strong anti-reductionist claim. Material objects are not inert residues from a dead sacred world. They are the remaining bodies of processes once lived intensely. To read them well is to sense the movement they continue to store.

In this sense antiquity externalized inner science without surrendering it completely. The wisdom entered matter, but matter did not exhaust the wisdom.
