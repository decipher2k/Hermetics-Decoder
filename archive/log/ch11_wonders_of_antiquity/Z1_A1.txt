Central statements
- The chapter treats marvels of antiquity as survivals of lost sciences rather than mere curiosities.
- Ever-burning lamps, oracles, and monumental works are presented as material vessels of preserved intelligence.
- The central issue is not whether wonders astonish, but what kind of knowledge made them possible.

Operative qualities
Durability, concealment, technical-sacred craft, mediated presence, and preservation through matter.

Reformulation

This chapter presents the wonders of antiquity as traces of disciplined knowledge embedded in matter, ritual mechanism, and constructed form. Hall is not chiefly interested in marvels as entertainment. He treats them as evidence that ancient cultures knew how to preserve force, meaning, or function in ways later ages only half remember.

The discussion of perpetual lamps establishes the chapter's first pattern. Flame becomes the emblem of continuity maintained inside sealed darkness. Whether one stresses chemistry, priestcraft, or hidden fuel, the point is the same: matter can be prepared so that visible light outlasts the ordinary term of consumption.

The account of Greek oracles introduces a second pattern. Sacred places, fumes, vessels, trees, priestesses, and ritual preparations combine to form apparatuses of mediation. Prophecy does not appear as arbitrary magic, but as a managed threshold where human consciousness is altered so that another order of speech may enter.

The Seven Wonders gather these lines into monumental permanence. Architecture, sculpture, and sacred engineering are shown as the large-scale equivalent of the lamp and the oracle: forms in which hidden doctrine survives materially. The chapter therefore reads antiquity as a civilization able to store intelligence in objects, places, and proportions.
