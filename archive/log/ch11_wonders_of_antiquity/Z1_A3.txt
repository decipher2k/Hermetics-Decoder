Extraction

Central statements
- Hall continually contrasts skeptical dismissal with initiatory interpretation.
- The marvelous object is a remnant whose real use is obscured by time.
- Loss of explanatory tradition increases the aura of miracle.

Operative qualities
Remnancy, obscured function, interpretive recovery, reverence before fragments, and survival through opacity.

Reformulation

The chapter is structured by a recurring tension between surviving artifact and lost explanation. Medieval polemicists blame devils, rationalizers invent tricks, and antiquarians dispute details, yet none of these reactions dissolves the more important fact that something durable has remained. Hall repeatedly shifts emphasis from argument over plausibility to recognition of remnant intelligence.

This is especially evident in the accounts of perpetual lamps discovered in tombs and sealed chambers. Whether every report is historically exact matters less than the symbolic pattern they express: ancient peoples sought to preserve light against darkness, time, and death. The lamp becomes an emblem of knowledge surviving burial.

The oracles show the same pattern differently. Later observers can describe fumes, tripods, kettles, or strange rites, but the inner logic that once made them transparent has receded. What survives is mechanism without full key, and therefore mechanism appears miraculous.

The great monuments belong to the same order of remnants. They stand after the civilizations that built them have collapsed, forcing later generations to infer, wonder, and reconstruct. Hall treats this opacity not as failure, but as proof that doctrine can remain hidden inside enduring form.
