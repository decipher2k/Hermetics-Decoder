Extraction

Central statements
- The chapter portrays wonder as a sign of broken but not erased literacy.
- Later observers still feel the force of ancient works even when they no longer understand the full design.
- Awe becomes the residue of partially unread symbolic function.

Operative qualities
Awe, unread remainder, symbolic pressure, partial legibility, and persistence beyond explanation.

Reformulation

Wonder in this chapter is not ignorance alone. It is the experience produced when a form continues to exert pressure after the interpretive key has weakened. Ever-burning lamps, prophetic caverns, speaking trees, reverberating kettles, and immense sacred monuments all provoke awe because they are more intelligible than chance yet less transparent than ordinary use.

Hall repeatedly leans into that interval. He neither dismisses marvels into fraud nor reduces them to sentimental piety. Instead he treats astonishment as evidence that the artifact still transmits something of its originating order.

This means the chapter belongs to the book's broader labor of recovering literacy in symbolic remains. To decipher a wonder is not to strip it of mystery, but to understand more exactly how mystery had been housed in craft, rite, and proportion.

The marvel, then, is a pedagogical remnant. It confronts the later world with surviving intensity and asks whether the observer can move from amazement to disciplined interpretation.
