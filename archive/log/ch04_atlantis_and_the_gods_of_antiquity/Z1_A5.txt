Central statements
- The chapter turns from Atlantis to the dying-god pattern without abandoning the same logic of loss and return.

Operative qualities
Descent, mourning, symbolic death, restoration, and ritualized regeneration.

Reformulation

The chapter's turn to dying-god myths is not a digression. It extends the same pattern established by Atlantis: loss, descent, fragmentation, and the guarded possibility of restoration.

In Tammuz, Adonis, Atys, the Cabiri, Odin, and related rites, death becomes ritual knowledge. The god descends, is torn, lost, buried, lamented, or suspended, and from this passage comes regeneration, illumination, or salvation.

The myths are not treated as mere seasonal folklore. Hall reads them as preserved Mystery formulas. The candidate participates in the god's death in order to awaken to a deeper form of life.

This gives the chapter a new center. What Atlantis is at the scale of civilization, the dying-god is at the scale of the initiate: fall, concealment, ordeal, and possible return.
