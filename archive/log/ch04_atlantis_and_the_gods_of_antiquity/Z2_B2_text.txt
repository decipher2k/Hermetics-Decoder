The chapter now reads as an account of active origin. Atlantis is important because it shows how a sacred center can vanish materially and yet continue to act through symbols, myths, emigrant priesthoods, and ritual forms.

Its destruction is therefore redistributive. What had been concentrated in one civilizational center is scattered into many traditions. Sea-born teachers, serpent emblems, sacred cities, pyramidal forms, and missionary memories all become signs of broken continuity.

The dying-god myths continue this same law on the ritual level. Tammuz, Adonis, Atys, the Cabiri, and Odin all preserve regenerative passage: descent, wounding, burial, mourning, and restored life. The initiate enters these dramas to learn that loss can become the medium of recovery.

The chapter's strongest claim is that catastrophe does not terminate wisdom. It compels wisdom to survive by compression, repetition, and symbolic migration.
