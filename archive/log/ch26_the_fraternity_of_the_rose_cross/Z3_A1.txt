The third cycle resolves the chapter around a central law: esoteric truth often persists most powerfully where its public trace is weakest. Hall uses the Rosicrucian controversy to argue that hidden traditions cannot be understood by literalism alone. Their reality may be distributed across manifestoes, symbols, suspected lineages, reforming influence, and transformed persons rather than concentrated in a single institution visible to historians.

This is why the four postulates matter. Together they form a graduated hermeneutic of secrecy. The historical, allegorical, fictional, and transcendental readings are not simply rivals; they mark different levels on which an esoteric current can exist and act. Rosicrucianism may be simultaneously literary in form, symbolic in doctrine, historical in effects, and supra-historical in aspiration.

The chapter's real accomplishment is to make that layered possibility intelligible without dissolving into vagueness.
