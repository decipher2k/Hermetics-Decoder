What becomes decisive is the distinction between appearance and vehicle. A document may be pseudonymous yet preservative. A society may be partially spurious yet carry fragments of a genuine current. A symbol may outlive the school that first organized it. A later order may inherit forms while losing keys. Hall's treatment of Rosicrucianism depends on this logic of uneven survival.

That is why the chapter keeps returning to manifestoes, strange letters, sealed names, anonymous visitations, and suspected affiliations. The Brotherhood operates through vehicles that do not fully reveal it. Hidden wisdom survives by overdetermining its containers and allowing uncertainty to guard the center.

The Rosicrucian problem therefore becomes exemplary for the whole book: symbols must often be trusted more than surface institutional narratives.
