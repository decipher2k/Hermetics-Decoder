The second cycle clarifies that Hall is using Rosicrucianism to think about legitimacy under esoteric conditions. Ordinary historiography expects names, dates, buildings, and institutional continuity. The Rosicrucian phenomenon frustrates those expectations by appearing through manifestoes, suspected affiliations, symbolic literature, rumors of adepts, and a doctrine that authentic members remain unknown. The chapter thereby asks what counts as evidence when a tradition intentionally withholds visibility.

This does not mean that anything mysterious is automatically true. Hall repeatedly marks the problem of imposture, forged claims, and romantic projection. But he also refuses the opposite error of assuming that what escapes documentary capture must be fictitious. The Rosicrucian enigma becomes a test case in how secrecy alters the terms of historical judgment.

The four postulates are therefore methodological instruments as much as content. They prevent naive literalism and naive dismissal alike.
