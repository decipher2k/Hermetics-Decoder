Hall opens this chapter by attacking the easy dismissal of alchemy as collective delusion. If emperors, physicians, philosophers, theologians, and practical experimenters over many centuries affirmed the possibility of transmutation, then ridicule is not an adequate answer. The issue is not whether every claim is proven but whether an entire tradition populated by disciplined and often brilliant minds can be swept aside as mere fraud or madness. Hall therefore shifts the defense of alchemy from abstract theory to exemplary lives. He surveys those who dedicated themselves to the Hermetic art in order to show what sort of intelligence, patience, daring, and spiritual ambition the subject attracted.

The chapter begins by reminding the reader that alchemy once stood close to political and intellectual power. Hall cites the repeal under William and Mary of the old English statute treating multiplication of metals as a felony, a repeal justified on the grounds that skilled practitioners were already improving metals and extracting gold and silver. He also recounts Franz Hartmann's report of Wenzel Seiler transmuting tin into gold before Emperor Leopold I, even partly converting a silver medal whose altered section was preserved as evidence. Such anecdotes are not offered as final scientific proof but as testimony that the claim of transmutation was historically taken seriously by governments, courts, and witnesses, not merely by cloistered dreamers.

Paracelsus is the first great exemplar. Born Philippus Aureolus Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim, he embodies for Hall the fusion of medicine, occult philosophy, and alchemical experiment. Trained early through his family's association with medicine and chemistry, he traveled widely, perhaps into Asia, and is said to have received Hermetic secrets from Arabian adepts. On returning to Europe he attacked established medical orthodoxy, denounced sterile dependence on ancient authorities, criticized apothecaries, and insisted on observation, experience, and nature rather than inherited dogma. His temper and personal irregularities made him an easy target, yet Hall emphasizes his remarkable cures, prolific writings, and reforming energy. Even his enemies, by resorting to slander and perhaps assassination, testify to the force with which he threatened the professional order of his time. In Paracelsus, alchemy appears not as escapist fantasy but as a living reform of science grounded in a larger philosophy of nature.

Other figures appear as surrounding witnesses to the same tradition. Albertus Magnus is recalled as theologian, philosopher, astrologer, and reputed constructor of an artificial speaking automaton, a man in whom scholastic authority and occult experiment meet without embarrassment. Solomon Trismosin, a shadowy early instructor of Paracelsus, represents the wandering adept who claims to have gained the formula of transmutation and to have prolonged life through alchemical knowledge. His saying that all that is without is also within becomes one of the chapter's governing maxims, because Hall wants the reader to understand alchemy as a correspondence between man and cosmos rather than a merely furnace-bound trick.

Raymond Lully supplies a different pattern: the courtier turned penitent, missionary, scholar, and alchemist. Hall recounts the famous episode in which Lully's worldly passion is shattered by the revelation of bodily corruption, leading him to renunciation and religious quest. He studies Arabic, seeks conversion of the infidel, encounters Arnold of Villa Nova, and learns the secret of transmutation. His later dealings with Edward of England and the legendary making of vast quantities of gold in the Tower reveal a recurring Hall theme: rulers desire alchemical wealth, but the adept insists that such power be subordinated to a higher end. Edward's bad faith converts patronage into imprisonment, and Lully escapes to continue his perilous missionary life until his martyr-like end. In this life, alchemy appears not as greed but as a knowledge repeatedly distorted by political appetite.

Nicholas Flammel provides the classic story of revelation through a mysterious book. A Parisian scrivener by trade, he acquires the Book of Abraham the Jew, written on bark-like leaves and filled with strange letters, symbolic images, and guarded instructions. For years he studies it unsuccessfully, copies its images, and seeks interpreters until he encounters a learned physician, Master Canches, who recognizes the tradition behind the emblems. Though the adept dies before the journey ends, Flammel receives enough guidance to continue the work. With his wife Perrenelle he solves the remaining riddle, achieves transmutation, and afterward hides the doctrine in hieroglyphic figures set up in Paris. Hall lingers over Flammel because his story captures several recurring features of Hermetic lore: sacred texts fall into unlikely hands, symbols conceal what words will not reveal, an adept appears briefly to unlock the code, and success imposes the duty of re-veiling the arcanum rather than exposing it plainly.

Count Bernard of Treviso represents the virtue of endurance carried to the limit. Beginning his quest in youth, he spends fortune, reputation, and decades in pursuit of the Philosopher's Stone. Charlatans mislead him, his family thinks him insane, and whole years vanish in futile processes such as calcining egg shells and distilling alcohol. Yet Hall does not present Bernard as a cautionary fool. Instead he becomes the image of perseverance purified by disappointment. Only in old age, near his seventy-sixth year, does success arrive, and with it the conviction that the result justifies the labor of a lifetime. Bernard's emphasis on dissolution through mercury as the supreme secret shows that true alchemy requires patient obedience to principles rather than fascination with glittering results.

Taken together, these biographies make Hall's broader argument. The art of alchemy must be judged partly by the kind of human lives it engenders: reformers like Paracelsus, penitents like Lully, interpreters of symbolic books like Flammel, and indefatigable seekers like Bernard. Hall does not deny the presence of frauds, myths, and uncertainties, but he insists that beneath them stands a persistent discipline joined to medicine, theology, moral struggle, symbolic literacy, and the hope of transforming both matter and man. Alchemy, in this chapter, survives not merely as a claim about metals but as a demanding vocation proved by the seriousness of its exponents.
