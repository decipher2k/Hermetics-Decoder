This chapter validates alchemy by way of its human vessels. Hall does not ask the reader to accept the art on credulity alone; he asks him to consider the stature, discipline, sacrifice, and practical intelligence of those who pursued it. Paracelsus stands as the chapter's clearest example of Hermetic science turned toward medical and social reform. Flammel, Lully, and Bernard of Treviso show the complementary features of hidden books, secret teachers, repeated failure, perseverance, and late success. The chapter argues that the Great Work is credible because it repeatedly attracts and forms a recognizable type of seeker: patient, reforming, initiatory, and unwilling to separate material transmutation from inward change.
