Central statements
- The chapter's lasting force lies in making philosophy initiatory without irrationalism.
- Mystery, symbol, and miracle are ordered by discipline and proportion.
- Reason is elevated, not rejected.

Operative qualities
Initiatory reason, disciplined mystery, elevated intellect, and ordered transcendence.

Reformulation

At its strongest, the chapter shows that initiation and reason need not oppose one another. Pythagoras works with miracle-stories, symbolic sayings, sacred numbers, occult correspondences, and living heavens, yet everything is gathered under discipline, proportion, and intelligible structure.

This is why the chapter remains so important within the whole book. It offers one of Hall's clearest models of esotericism without irrationalism. Hidden truths are not arbitrary secrets; they are higher orders of relation requiring a trained intellect and a reformed life.

Reason therefore is not overthrown by mystery. It is purified, widened, and taught to operate symbolically.

The chapter can be read as a defense of elevated intelligence against both skepticism and credulity.
