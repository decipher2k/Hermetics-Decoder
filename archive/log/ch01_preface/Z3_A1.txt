Central statements
- The Preface has stabilized as a theory of custody through form.
- The author appears as servant of transmission, not proprietor of truth.
- Modernity is challenged chiefly for its narrowing of admissible reality.
- Reading is defined as an interior discipline.

Operative qualities
Custody, service, widening, and disciplined inwardness.

Reformulation

This book begins from the belief that enduring truth should not be carried in weakened form. The concealed philosophies of earlier ages have too often reached later readers in vessels too slight for their depth. I have therefore tried to prepare a book whose visible shape does not betray the seriousness of what it bears. The care given to beauty and order is part of that fidelity.

The manuscript belongs to years of writing, but writing was only the outward completion of a longer hidden labor. Sources had to be gathered, translations secured, relationships recognized, and structure discovered before the text could take final form. Footnotes were removed so that understanding could proceed without repeated interruption. References were drawn into the body of the work. A bibliography opens further paths, and the index provides orientation through difficult material.

I do not speak with infallibility, nor do I pretend to independence from those whose thought preceded mine. Ancient material survives in partial form, and the interpreter who forgets that limit turns fidelity into presumption. Some passages may seem bold, yet they have been set down with restraint and with an effort to preserve living intention rather than dead wording. Any error that remains is my own.

This work does not force the material into a private doctrine, and it does not erase real divergence for the sake of easy agreement.

It does, however, resist the narrowing assumptions of modern materialism. Its purpose is to reopen a wider horizon of life and understanding. The field is too large to admit perfect arrangement, yet related themes have been joined where they can illuminate one another.

Such material asks for more than literal reading. Language can indicate these realities, but cannot exhaust them. The reader must bring patience and inward responsiveness.

The book also carries the labor of many others: libraries that preserved, translators that reopened, editors that ordered, craftsmen that gave visible stability, and subscribers whose faith preceded completion.

For that reason gratitude is part of the truth of the work.

If the reader's perception is widened here, then the long making of the book will have continued its proper work.
