Central statements
- The book seeks worthy expression for enduring wisdom.
- The manuscript is the visible result of years of hidden preparation.
- The author disclaims final certainty and personal doctrinal ambition.
- The work positions itself against reductive modern assumptions.
- Comprehension requires both study and a subtler mode of attention.

Operative qualities
Preparation, visibility emerging from hidden labor, measured honesty, resistance to reduction, and disciplined receptivity.

Reformulation

The ancient world has been commented upon many times, yet the perennial truths entrusted to those commentaries have too often appeared in poor form. This volume attempts to give those truths a more adequate body. Its making has been expensive in effort and means, but the hope remains that the reader will feel the result as something more than mere information.

The writing began on the first day of 1926 and continued for more than two years. Even so, the visible manuscript rests on earlier invisible labor. Materials were being collected as early as 1921, and the structure of the book emerged clearly some years later. Footnotes were removed to keep the movement unbroken, while references were absorbed into the text itself. The bibliography is there for those who wish to go farther, and the index was designed so that difficult matter can still be approached through order.

Nothing here is offered as infallible, and nothing is offered as if untouched by prior sources. Ancient testimonies survive in fragments, which makes dogmatic confidence irresponsible. Some statements may appear bold or improbable, yet they are presented without reckless invention and with as much fidelity as possible to the inner intention of the original writers. Whatever errors remain are mine to bear.

I do not advance an ism of my own. I have not twisted sources to support prior convictions, nor have I tried to force peace upon doctrines that genuinely differ.

The book turns toward subjects that the modern mind often treats with contempt. Its deeper purpose is to open the reader to a conception of living beyond the restrictions of materialist thought. Because the material is large and difficult, not everything can be arranged perfectly, but related matters have been drawn together wherever possible.

English can carry much, yet it does not easily contain the finest shades of metaphysical insight. The reader must therefore supply an inward responsiveness if the older teachings are to become fully intelligible.

The work owes much to libraries and friends. Public institutions, university collections, Masonic libraries, and private helpers all contributed to its making, and many particular persons deserve gratitude for practical support.

Translation demanded more sustained effort than any other part of the research. Alfred Beri bore the burden of the German materials. Homer P. Earle translated several other languages. Rabbi Jacob M. Alkow revised the Hebrew passages, and many others helped with shorter pieces and checks.

The manuscript also depended on those who gave it literary and material form. Dr. C. B. Rowlingson ordered the text. Robert B. Tummonds, M. M. Saxton, J. Augustus Knapp, Frederick E. Keast, John Henry Nash, and C. E. Benson each helped make the physical book worthy of the thought it carries.

The response before publication was remarkable. Multiple editions found subscribers in advance because trusted supporters understood the work as something to be placed before serious readers rather than merely marketed.

For that reason I owe a special debt to the subscribers whose faith bore the financial risk.

May the reader gain from this book as I gained from writing it. The labor revealed truths, trained patience, shaped order, opened the crafts, and brought companions into view. Those gains are part of the gift the book now offers.
