Central statements
- Form matters because wisdom deserves worthy embodiment.
- Research is long, cumulative, and carefully mediated.
- The author opposes certainty that hardens beyond evidence.
- The work is comparative without becoming flattening or sectarian.
- Understanding requires cultivated intuition.

Operative qualities
Embodiment, patience, careful mediation, non-coercive synthesis, and inward readiness.

Reformulation

Many volumes have treated the hidden philosophical systems of the ancient world, but the living truths at their center have often been clothed in inadequate forms. This book is an effort to provide a more fitting embodiment for the insight of the sages whose thought fills its pages. The union of beauty and truth has been costly to bring about, yet I trust that its effect upon the reader will make that cost intelligible.

The composition of this volume began on January 1, 1926, and continued with little interruption for more than two years. The research behind it reaches further back. Source materials began to be assembled in 1921, and by 1924 the book's design was already set. Footnotes were omitted so that the movement of the text could remain clear. References and quotations were integrated into the body where they serve the argument directly. A bibliography has been included to direct future study, and a topical index has been prepared so that dense subject matter may remain accessible.

I make no claim to final authority and no boast of complete originality. The remains of ancient thought are too partial to support rigid declarations, and such declarations often do more harm than good. Some propositions in this work may appear fantastic, yet I have tried to avoid undisciplined speculation and to preserve the inward meaning of the original writers as faithfully as possible. Any errors that remain belong to me alone.

I have no wish to press an inherited text into the service of a private doctrine. Neither have I attempted to remove every contradiction among the world's religio-philosophic systems by force.

The theory informing this book stands against the prevailing tendencies of modern thought because it addresses subjects habitually dismissed by contemporary opinion. Its aim is to draw the reader toward a conception of life beyond the limits imposed by materialistic theology, philosophy, and science. The material is difficult and extensive, but related topics have been assembled together whenever order could be made to appear.

The English language, for all its power, remains imperfect when asked to carry subtle metaphysical insight. Anyone entering these subjects must therefore supply something more than literal reading. There must be a responsive faculty able to recognize meanings that ordinary terms cannot fully contain.

I owe thanks to numerous libraries and individuals whose generosity sustained this work. Public, university, Masonic, and private collections all contributed to the gathering of materials, and many friends lent books or offered indispensable assistance.

Translation was the heaviest single demand of the research. German sources were rendered over years through Alfred Beri's generosity. Homer P. Earle undertook the Latin, Italian, French, and Spanish materials. Rabbi Jacob M. Alkow revised the Hebrew text, while additional helpers supported shorter translations and checks.

Editorial order and physical execution also required uncommon skill. Dr. C. B. Rowlingson shaped literary disorder into usable form. Robert B. Tummonds, M. M. Saxton, J. Augustus Knapp, Frederick E. Keast, John Henry Nash, and C. E. Benson each contributed to the crafted life of the finished book.

The pre-publication response was exceptional. Entire editions were subscribed before completion, largely because devoted supporters treated the work as something to be entrusted to serious readers rather than merely sold as merchandise.

I remain indebted to the subscribers whose advance confidence made the publication of this volume possible.

My sincere hope is that readers may gain from this work as I gained from making it. The labor taught me truth, order, patience, craftsmanship, and friendship. Those are among the gifts the book now carries forward.
