Reformulation

This volume was conceived from the sense that ancient wisdom must be carried in forms equal to its gravity. Too often the philosophies of earlier ages have survived in diminished presentation. I have tried to fashion a book in which truth is not merely repeated but borne with dignity, so that beauty and meaning reinforce rather than weaken each other.

The manuscript belongs to the years of writing that began in 1926, but that visible labor arose from an earlier discipline of gathering and preparation. Materials had to be collected, compared, and arranged before they could become a coherent book. Footnotes were omitted so the reader could move through the argument more continuously. References were placed within the text itself. The bibliography remains as a guide outward, and the index serves as an aid through difficulty.

I do not claim certainty beyond the evidence, and I do not pretend that this work stands outside the sources it depends upon. Ancient writings come to us in fragments. To speak dogmatically where the evidence is partial is to betray the subject. Some claims may seem surprising, yet they have been advanced with caution and with an effort to preserve the original impulse of the material. The book may still contain errors, but those errors are mine.

I have not forced these materials into the service of a personal doctrine. Nor have I tried to erase meaningful differences merely to create a false harmony.

The whole enterprise stands opposed to the narrowing tendencies of modern thought, because it turns toward realities modernity often dismisses. Its aim is to enlarge the reader's sense of possible life and knowledge. The material cannot be completely systematized, but it has been grouped with deliberate care.

Because language strains under metaphysical subtlety, reading here requires more than literal comprehension. The reader must be able to feel implication, not only statement.

The work also depends on the help of many others. Libraries provided access. Scholars and friends extended practical support. Translators, editors, artists, and printers each carried the project across a threshold it could not cross alone.

Translation was among the greatest labors because the work could not be whole while large portions of its source material remained linguistically inaccessible. Production was equally important, since the material form had to remain worthy of the intellectual and spiritual seriousness of the content.

That seriousness was recognized in advance by subscribers whose support made publication possible.

For all of this assistance I remain deeply grateful.

If these pages succeed in reviving some deeper mode of reading in those who encounter them, then the long preparation of the book will have found its rightful fulfillment.
