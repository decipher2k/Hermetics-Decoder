Extraction

Central statements
- Worthy transmission requires proportion between idea and form.
- The book's structure is itself an interpretive act.
- The author affirms responsibility while refusing domination over the material.
- The reader must supply inward responsiveness.

Operative qualities
Proportion, interpretive ordering, responsibility, and responsive participation.

Reformulation

The impulse behind this book is simple: truths of enduring value should not continue to circulate in forms that diminish them. The hidden philosophies of the ancient world deserve a vessel proportionate to their depth. If this book has required great care in both content and presentation, that care is part of the truthfulness of the undertaking.

The written volume emerged over more than two years, yet the work began long before the first page of prose. Sources had to be gathered, plans had to mature, and relationships among scattered materials had to be recognized. Footnotes were set aside to protect continuity. References were woven into the body of the text so that learning could remain alive within the argument. A bibliography points outward toward further study, and the index serves as an instrument of orientation in dense terrain.

I claim neither complete originality nor immunity from error. Ancient doctrines reach us in partial form, and dogmatic confidence in the face of partial evidence is intellectually irresponsible. Some of what appears here may at first seem extravagant, but it has not been advanced as idle speculation. I have tried to keep close to the inner direction of the sources. Any fault that remains belongs to me.

No personal creed has been imposed upon these materials. I have neither twisted texts to confirm prior beliefs nor forced divergent traditions into counterfeit agreement.

The book resists modern contraction because it attends to realities that modern habits often dismiss in advance. Its purpose is to widen the field of possible understanding. Since the material is vast, the arrangement cannot be absolutely perfect, yet it has been shaped so that related themes can illuminate one another.

The language available to us is not fully adequate to the most subtle philosophical matters. A literal reader will therefore miss part of what is present. The text asks for a mind capable of inference, resonance, and inward perception.

This volume also rests on generosity. Collections were opened, books were lent, translations were undertaken, and skilled workers gave the manuscript order and material beauty.

The burden of translation was especially great, because without it much of the material would have remained closed. The burden of production was likewise great, because the book needed a physical form that did not trivialize what it contained.

Its publication further depended upon confidence in advance of completion.

I remain grateful to all who gave that confidence and that labor.

If the reader finds within these pages a widened sense of truth, then the long making of the book will have found its proper continuation.
