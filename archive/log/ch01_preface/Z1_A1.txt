Central statements
- The author presents the book as an attempt to give enduring wisdom a form worthy of its depth.
- The work is the result of long research, deliberate organization, and editorial shaping.
- The author refuses infallibility, rejects dogmatism, and resists forcing old doctrines into modern systems.
- The book is meant to open the reader to a way of understanding life that exceeds materialist habits of thought.
- Language is inadequate to the subject, so the reader must bring intuition as well as intellect.
- The work is made possible by a network of libraries, translators, editors, craftsmen, subscribers, and friends.

Operative qualities
Preservation, dignification, disciplined humility, anti-dogmatic fidelity, gratitude, and transmission through labor.

Tension and resonance with the whole
The Preface already stages the main tension of the book: truth must enter form without being reduced to form. It also introduces the whole work as a recovery of hidden continuity across scattered traditions.

Reformulation

Many books have commented on the concealed philosophies of the ancient world, yet the lasting truths they tried to carry have too often been presented in forms unworthy of them. This book is offered as an attempt to give those seers and sages a vessel equal to the substance of their thought. Bringing beauty and truth into one body has demanded unusual cost, but I believe the effect upon the reader's mind will justify that effort.

Work on this volume began on the first day of January, 1926, and continued with little interruption for more than two years. Much of the research, however, had already been done before the manuscript itself was written. The gathering of sources started in 1921, and three years later the design of the book took clear shape. For the sake of clarity, footnotes were removed and quotations or references were woven into the body of the text where they could function more naturally. The bibliography remains to guide further study, and the cross index has been prepared so the dense material may be approached more readily.

I do not claim that every statement here is beyond error, nor do I claim complete originality. The surviving writings of the ancients are too fragmentary to justify dogmatic certainty, and reckless certainty has damaged modern philosophy more than it has helped it. Some assertions in these pages may seem extravagant at first encounter, yet I have tried to avoid careless speculation and to present the material in the spirit of its original sources rather than as a rigid literal reconstruction. I accept responsibility for whatever mistakes remain, and by doing so I hope to avoid the charge of borrowing without acknowledgment that has so often shadowed writers in this field.

I have no private doctrine that I am trying to impose. I have not twisted older texts to support a preconceived system, nor have I tried to force agreement where real differences remain among religious and philosophical traditions.

The governing idea of this book stands sharply against the habits of modern thought, because it concerns subjects openly mocked by the self-assured voices of the twentieth century. Its real aim is to introduce the reader to a possibility of living that lies beyond materialistic theology, philosophy, and science. The mass of difficult material gathered here cannot be arranged with perfect elegance, yet related matters have been brought together wherever possible.

Even a language as rich as English lacks terms fully suited to abstract and interior philosophical realities. Because of this, anyone who wishes to understand the ancient mystery teachings must bring an intuitive grasp for meanings that cannot be perfectly carried by ordinary words.

Although many of the books cited in the bibliography are from my own library, I owe genuine thanks to the public and private collections that supported this work, especially the libraries of San Francisco, Los Angeles, Berkeley, the Scottish Rite, the Mechanics' Library, and the Krotona Theosophical Library. I am likewise indebted to many individuals who offered books, guidance, and practical help.

Translation proved the greatest single labor in the research process. German materials were translated over nearly three years through the generosity of Alfred Beri. The Latin, Italian, French, and Spanish passages were translated by Homer P. Earle. The Hebrew text was edited by Rabbi Jacob M. Alkow, while many others assisted with shorter translations and corrections.

The editorial shaping of the book was supervised by Dr. C. B. Rowlingson, who repeatedly brought order out of disorder. Technical and production difficulties were handled with equal devotion by Robert B. Tummonds, M. M. Saxton, J. Augustus Knapp, Frederick E. Keast, John Henry Nash, and C. E. Benson. Their combined labor helped make the book not only legible but worthy in form of the ideas it bears.

The sale of the book before publication was extraordinary. The first edition was fully subscribed long before the manuscript reached the printer, and later editions were also taken before the finished volume appeared. Much of the credit belongs to Maud F. Galigher and to friends who distributed the work not as a commercial object alone but as something to be placed into the hands of those prepared to value it.

I am deeply indebted to the many subscribers whose advance faith made publication possible. The expense of such a work lay beyond my personal means, and those who supported it did so with trust rather than certainty.

My hope is that every reader may profit from this book as I have profited from writing it. The labor taught me truth, order, patience, craft, and friendship. In that spirit I leave the work to its readers, trusting that what was slowly shaped into visible form may now widen into further understanding.
