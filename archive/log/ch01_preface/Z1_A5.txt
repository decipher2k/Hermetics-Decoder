Extraction

Central statements
- Wisdom needs a vessel equal to its worth.
- The book is the fruit of sustained collection, translation, arrangement, and craft.
- Intellectual honesty requires renouncing both infallibility and distortion.
- The work asks the reader to step beyond literalism.
- Publication itself becomes an act of collective trust.

Operative qualities
Vessel-making, fidelity, inward tact, collaboration, and trust.

Reformulation

This book begins from the conviction that enduring wisdom deserves a worthy vessel. Many have written on ancient hidden philosophy, but the truths themselves have too often appeared in worn or insufficient dress. I have tried to bring beauty and truth into one form, trusting that the effort spent on that union will repay itself in the reader's experience.

The text was written over more than two years beginning in 1926, but its foundation lies in earlier years of research and collection. By 1924 the plan had become clear. Footnotes were stripped away so the page might move with less interruption, while references were absorbed into the body of the work. A bibliography remains for those who wish to continue the search, and the index exists so that difficult matter does not remain inaccessible.

I do not claim perfect certainty, nor do I present these pages as wholly new. The surviving words of antiquity do not permit dogmatic finality. Some statements here may appear strange, yet they are not meant as idle speculation. I have tried to preserve the living intention of the sources rather than imprison them in mere literal repetition. If faults remain, they are mine.

No personal doctrine is being forced upon the material. I have not bent old writings to prove what I already believed, and I have not disguised real differences among traditions under artificial harmony.

The book moves against modern habits because it concerns realities that modern certainty often dismisses before understanding them. Its aim is to invite the reader into a broader conception of life than materialist systems permit. The material is difficult and cannot be perfectly ordered, but it has been grouped with care.

Because language is imperfect, especially in the region of subtle philosophy, the reader must participate inwardly. Mere literal reading is not enough.

This work also rests on generosity. Libraries, scholars, helpers, and friends provided access, materials, and practical support. Without them the book would be thinner in both substance and reach.

Translation was the greatest labor within the research. German, Latin, Italian, French, Spanish, and Hebrew materials all required devoted help, and that help was given generously.

Editorial and material form required equal devotion. Order had to be drawn from disorder, text fitted into space, images prepared, printing refined, and design brought into harmony with content.

The extraordinary advance sale of the book showed that others believed in the work before it could justify itself in finished form.

To those subscribers I owe profound thanks, since their trust made publication possible.

If the reader receives from this book even part of what I received in making it, the labor will have been worthwhile. It taught truth, patience, order, craft, and friendship, and I release it in gratitude.
