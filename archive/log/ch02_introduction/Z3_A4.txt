Reformulation

The Introduction increasingly defines philosophy as hidden-source literacy. It trains the mind to read visible doctrines, schools, and forms with awareness that they may derive from a deeper and partially concealed origin.

This makes sense of the many definitions of philosophy. Each definition marks one attempt to describe the movement from appearance toward source.

Its traditional branches become tools in that movement. Inquiry into being, thought, action, soul, knowledge, and beauty all become ways of learning source-consciousness.

Ancient thinkers understood this. Modern thought often lost it by treating schools as self-sufficient rather than derivative.

The chapter's historical survey therefore teaches doubled reading. On one level, each school is a real historical formation. On another, it is a residue of more primary questions and experiences. This is true of elemental, mathematical, rational, atomistic, moral, ascetic, hedonic, skeptical, stoic, systemic, empirical, intuitive, evolutionary, and pragmatic traditions alike.

The same doubled reading applies to the chapter's images. They are not merely illustrations, but symbolic residues carrying compressed doctrine.

The turning claim of the chapter is that Greek philosophy itself must be read that way. It is both an historical achievement and a derivative articulation of older sacred inheritances.

The Mysteries name the hidden matrix in which those inheritances were guarded and transmitted.

Secrecy explains why so much remains indirect.

Symbolism explains how so much still remains present.

The reader is therefore being retrained. To continue with the book, one must learn to read public doctrine and symbolic residue together, as two surfaces of a single concealed continuity.
