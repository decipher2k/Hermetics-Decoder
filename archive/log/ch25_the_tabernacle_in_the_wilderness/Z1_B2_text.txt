This chapter presents the Tabernacle as a portable mystery structure in which cosmology, anthropology, and worship are fused. Hall treats the Mosaic sanctuary not as a merely historical tent for sacrifice but as an initiatory edifice derived in part from Egyptian wisdom and shaped to preserve universal doctrine in measured space. Its courts, veils, vessels, garments, numbers, and materials are all intelligible only when read as symbols of invisible law.

The movement through the sanctuary is the chapter's governing form. At the outer threshold stands sacrifice, where the lower nature must be offered up rather than merely represented by an animal victim. At the laver comes purification, the cleansing required before any deeper participation. In the holy place stand the lamp of ordered illumination, the bread of sustained and measured life, and the altar of incense through which articulated offering rises upward. Beyond the veil lies the hidden chamber of concentrated presence, where the ark gathers law, covenant, and the overshadowing powers into a still center beyond ordinary access.

Everything in the chapter reinforces the claim that sacred architecture mirrors both the world and man. The three divisions correspond to levels of reality and of consciousness. The fourfold veil recalls the elements. The seven lamps, twelve loaves, and twelve stones bind the sanctuary to planetary and zodiacal order. Gold, silver, and brass disclose degrees of refinement, while robes, breastplate, bells, crown, and oracle stones transform the High Priest into a living emblem of cosmic correspondence. Yet Hall's final emphasis is ethical: no ornament guarantees access. Before the highest approach the priest must stand stripped of outward magnificence, clothed only in virtue. The true Tabernacle is therefore the human being rightly ordered so that the divine may dwell within it.
