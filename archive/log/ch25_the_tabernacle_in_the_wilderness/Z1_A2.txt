The first stable insight is that the Tabernacle is architectonic doctrine. Its dimensions, divisions, curtains, pillars, metals, and furnishings are not decorative additions to worship but the teaching itself given spatial form. Hall insists that the sanctuary was constructed as a miniature universe and as a symbolic body so that ritual movement through it would become an enacted philosophy.

The second stable insight is that the chapter is ordered by graded inwardness. One begins in the outer court among sacrifice and washing, proceeds into the chamber of light, bread, and incense, and finally approaches the hidden chamber accessible only under strict conditions. This progression is psychological and ontological at once: the soul moves from gross purification through ordered life and disciplined offering toward an interior center beyond ordinary access.

The third stable insight is that sacred office here means inward capability. The robes, gems, bells, flowers, crown, Urim and Thummim, and the breastplate all signify powers of perception, receptivity, balanced judgment, and cosmic correspondence. Hall repeatedly returns to the claim that no outward vestment avails by itself; before the final approach the High Priest must be stripped of ornament and stand clothed only in virtue.
