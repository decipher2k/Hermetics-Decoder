From this final perspective, the Tabernacle is a doctrine of inhabitation. God dwells among men when a suitable order is prepared. The sanctuary materializes that order for a wandering people, but the deeper implication is inward and perennial: the human being becomes a true dwelling only by reproducing within himself the same relations of sacrifice, cleansing, illumination, nourishment, articulation, veiling, and obedient silence.

This also explains why Hall links the Tabernacle to Masonry, the Pyramid, and other initiatory architectures. He is tracing a transhistorical grammar of sacred building. Whenever man constructs a form that mirrors the structure of the worlds and disciplines consciousness through passage, division, and symbol, he recreates the same essential operation.

The chapter is therefore one of the book's clearest statements that sacred architecture is spiritual engineering. It trains the candidate by arranging space according to truth.
