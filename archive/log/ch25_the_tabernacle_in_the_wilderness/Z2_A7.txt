The second cycle stabilizes the chapter as a master statement about sacred ordering. The Tabernacle is a portable cosmos, a human diagram, a graded path, and a test of moral fitness. Its instruments are stages of transformation, its garments are visible powers, and its veils are protections that preserve the reality of inward difference.

The chapter's decisive lesson is that divine contact cannot be separated from preparation. The sanctuary's entire arrangement denies immediacy without discipline. Access is always mediated by sacrifice, cleansing, harmonization, truthfulness, and surrender. The High Priest can represent the people only because he embodies this order, and even he is admitted only when stripped of all merely external distinction.

Hall therefore uses the Tabernacle to reassert a universal doctrine: the true temple is built whenever human life is arranged according to cosmic law and the innermost chamber is reserved for the presence that cannot be coerced. Sacred form is justified when it produces inward correspondence.
