The final convergence yields a stable reading. The Tabernacle in the wilderness is a portable temple of correspondences whose purpose is to teach how the divine may dwell among men without ceasing to transcend them. It is built as a miniature universe, read as a diagram of the sanctified human being, and traversed as an initiatory path from coarse offering to hidden presence.

Its furniture, numbers, materials, and vestments all belong to that one pedagogical aim. The outer court stages sacrifice and purification; the holy place orders light, nourishment, and ascent; the inner chamber concentrates law and overshadowed presence. The priesthood embodies the same structure in living form, yet the climax of the chapter insists that no robe, jewel, or office can replace inward worth. At the threshold of the holy of holies, symbolic glory must yield to spiritual reality.

Hall's larger claim is that sacred form is justified when it reproduces cosmic law and disciplines the soul into receptivity. The true Tabernacle is therefore neither merely historical nor merely architectural. It is the ordered human vessel in which the divine may be encountered through reverent preparation, exact correspondence, and purified intention.
