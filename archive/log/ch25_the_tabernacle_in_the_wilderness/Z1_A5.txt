The chapter's force depends on its double refusal: it refuses literalism and it refuses empty allegory. The Tabernacle is not merely historical furniture, but neither is it a set of arbitrary metaphors. Hall treats it as an operative symbolic machine in which architecture, ritual, and anthropology converge. The sanctuary means because it is constructed in the image of processes that genuinely govern life.

This makes the Holy of Holies decisive. The innermost chamber is empty of ordinary multiplicity and holds only the ark, the law, and the overshadowing cherubim. Presence is concentrated where form is most reduced. The logic is apophatic as well as ceremonial: the nearer one comes to the center, the less cluttered representation becomes.

The final warning regarding the High Priest's vulnerability strengthens this. Unclean entry destroys. The chain tied to the priest's foot dramatizes that inner approach is real risk, not theatrical symbolism. The chapter's initiation is therefore moral before it is visionary. The sanctuary reveals that the divine cannot be manipulated by costume, status, or knowledge without character.
