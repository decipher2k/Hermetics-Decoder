The symbols of the High Priest now stand out as a concentrated summary of the chapter. The shoulder stones carry the tribes as burdens of ordered responsibility. The breastplate gathers multiplicity into a harmonized field capable of oracular illumination. The bells and pomegranates join sound and fecundity, warning and abundance. The triple crown and flower-cup above the head present the priest as ruler across levels and as a vessel receptive to descending life.

What matters is that these are not decorations appended to sanctity after the fact. They are visible statements about what a sanctified agent must be. The priest is configured as a mediator in whom cosmic order, communal representation, and inward receptivity coincide. In that sense he is a human ark, a body adapted for contact.

Yet the chapter refuses to let these emblems become a fetish. The decisive act is the removal of glory before entry into the highest chamber. Hall uses this to insist that every symbolic investiture is preparatory and provisional. The divine presence receives only what is inwardly true.
