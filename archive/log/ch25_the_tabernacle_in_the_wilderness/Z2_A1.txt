The second cycle clarifies that the Tabernacle is not just symbolic space but enacted metaphysics. Hall uses it to show how divine pattern descends into measurable form without losing its higher reference. Because of this, architectural exactness matters: proportions, placements, materials, and furnishings are themselves doctrinal. To traverse the sanctuary is to move through a condensed account of creation, embodiment, discipline, and return.

This pass also sharpens the relation between outer rite and inner labor. Hall criticizes merely external sacrifice by translating every furnishing into an inward demand. The altar is the combustion of coarseness, the laver the removal of stain, the lights the ordered awakening of perception, the bread the sustaining pattern of life, the incense the ascent of refined utterance, and the ark the concealed law around which the rest must be arranged. The sanctuary is therefore an instrument for interior education.

The chapter's portability becomes philosophically important here. A fixed temple would localize sacred order, but a transportable one declares that the true dwelling of divinity is reproducible wherever a people or a person carries the pattern faithfully. The move from place to pattern is one of the chapter's deepest implications.
