The ark stands at the chapter's deepest center. It houses law, bears memory, serves as throne, and is overshadowed by powers that indicate proximity without possession. This makes it the perfect figure for Hall's theology of presence: the divine is communicative but not capturable, enthroned amid symbols yet never reducible to them. Presence descends between the cherubim, upon the mercy seat, in the interval guarded by form and silence.

Around this center the entire sanctuary can be understood as preparation for right relation. Every outer act exists to make one capable of that interval. Sacrifice removes coarseness, washing removes stain, lamps order perception, bread stabilizes life, incense rectifies expression, vestments school the body in correspondence, and the final stripping abolishes any claim that symbolic office itself compels the divine.

The chapter therefore unites ceremonial richness with radical humility. The more exact the form, the less license for ego. Holy order is not a means of control but a discipline of receptivity.
