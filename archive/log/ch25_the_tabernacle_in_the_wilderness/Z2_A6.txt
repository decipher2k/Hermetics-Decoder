The chapter can now be condensed into an initiatory grammar. First, matter must be ordered and consecrated. Second, the lower self must be surrendered. Third, impurity must be washed away. Fourth, light, nourishment, and right utterance must be established. Fifth, symbolic powers must be borne responsibly. Sixth, even sacred status must be renounced. Seventh, presence may then be approached in fear, silence, and obedience.

Seen this way, the Tabernacle is not an antiquarian curiosity but a process model for the formation of a fit vessel. Hall's interest in every measurement and ornament is justified because each is a mnemonic for one phase or law of transformation. The sanctuary teaches by requiring a person to think spatially, ritually, materially, and morally all at once.

The chapter's continued relevance in the wider book is that it shows how ancient religion safeguards high doctrine through embodied form. What later ages call ceremony is, at its best, an engineering of consciousness.
