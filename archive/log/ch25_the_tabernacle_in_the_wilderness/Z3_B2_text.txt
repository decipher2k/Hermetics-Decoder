This chapter treats the Tabernacle as a traveling sanctuary built to teach through form what cannot be safely entrusted to literal explanation alone. Hall argues that much in the first books of the Old Testament was shaped by Egyptian initiatory religion, and that the Mosaic system preserved, under Hebrew forms, a body of symbolic doctrine learned from older priestly schools. Whether Moses himself wrote the Pentateuch matters less here than the kind of figure he was. Hall rejects the reduction that makes Moses nothing but a solar myth and instead reads him as an initiate, a man authorized by wisdom-centers to carry sacred law to a people not yet capable of receiving it in naked metaphysical language.

In that sense even the name Moses is treated as initiatory. It denotes not merely an individual biography, but one who has entered the school of hidden knowledge and returns from it charged with teaching the ignorant about the gods, the soul, and the lawful order of life. The stories tied to Moses, his rescue, adoption, education in Egypt, conflict with empire, and later guidance of Israel, are therefore read on two levels at once. They may be historical in part, but they also preserve ritual and cosmic patterns familiar to the Mystery schools. Hall's larger point is that the religion founded around such a figure would almost inevitably carry Egyptian esoteric method inside its visible institutions.

Egypt itself appears here as the immense forgotten source behind later sacred forms. Its priests believed their wisdom far older than ordinary memory, and Hall recasts that claim by linking Egypt to an even more remote Atlantean inheritance. The historical details remain uncertain, but the function is clear: Egyptian religion is presented as a vessel of profound symbolic science, and that science did not die with Egypt because it moved outward through later peoples. Moses becomes for Israel what Odin was for the North and Quetzalcoatl for Mexico, a founder who gives a wandering people not only rules of worship but a patterned school of transformation.

The Tabernacle Mysteries therefore stand at the center of the chapter. To the crowd, the Tabernacle was only a holy enclosure where offerings were made and rites performed. To the instructed, it was a reduced universe, a transportable temple of learning, a visible arrangement through which the relations between God, world, and man could be contemplated. Hall insists that Scripture itself only makes full sense if one grants the existence of unwritten keys. Moses, he says, entrusted oral interpretations to chosen initiates, and these keys later came to be known as the Qabbalah. Without them, the written law remains a shell whose inner logic is easily missed.

This is why the chapter lingers on keys, gates, and custodians. Janus with his double face, Peter with the keys of heaven, the papal gold and silver keys, all are brought in to suggest one continuing principle: sacred writing contains more than it states, and some power of interpretation must unlock it. The Tabernacle itself follows the same law. Like the Egyptian temples from which it is said to have been adapted, it is a miniature cosmos. The solar system is imagined as a vast temple of initiation, and human life becomes a procession through it. Birth is entrance, experience is ritual wandering, death is the great veil, and successful passage through that veil is the final initiation. Hence Socrates' last request for a sacrificial cock is read as a final acknowledgment that he, too, is about to undergo the great rite.

Everything in the sanctuary is therefore arranged to instruct. Life itself is the mystery, and temple, ritual, and symbol merely teach the candidate how to move through it without remaining trapped at its outward surface. The Ark, the altars, the curtains, and the priestly actions all belong to a larger pedagogy. Hall notes that the Jewish Ark itself resembles Egyptian prototypes carried on poles by priests, which means the visible forms of Hebrew worship already testify to continuity with older sacred art. Josephus becomes important here because he preserves a symbolic reading that later literalism often ignored.

The construction of the Tabernacle is then described as deliberate sacred architecture. Moses appoints Besaleel of Judah and Aholiab of Dan to oversee the work, and Hall immediately reads even their tribal symbols esoterically. Judah's lion and Dan's eagle or serpent are linked with alchemical and zodiacal powers, implying that the builders themselves personify elemental and initiatory functions. The sanctuary they construct is not merely where God visits man; it is also an image of the soul-body in which divine presence can dwell. As the tribes camp around the tent, so the many elements of human nature gather around the inmost spiritual center.

The scriptural command to build the sanctuary from gold, silver, brass, dyed fabrics, skins, wood, oil, spices, and stones is therefore more than a list of materials. Each material expresses a level of being and a mode of value. The courtyard forms a measured rectangle, open to the east so that the rising sun can enter, and its linen curtains on brazen pillars establish a boundary between ordinary existence and ordered sacred space. At the entry stands the Altar of Burnt Offerings, and farther inward the Laver of Purification. Already the candidate's movement is moralized: first sacrifice, then cleansing, then entry into deeper understanding.

The Tabernacle proper, placed at the center of the court, is interpreted as a structure of threefold inwardness. The common way of dividing it into the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies is supplemented by Josephus' suggestion of a further subdivision, because the chapter wants to emphasize stages of access rather than a flat binary. The curtains, pillars, and veils are never only decorative. The four pillars before the veil suggest the four elements. The seven pillars at the entrance allude to the senses, the vowels, and the sacred sevenfold order by which manifestation becomes articulate. The coverings of linen, goat hair, dyed skins, and dark outer protection are similarly read as successive sheaths, each preserving and concealing the more precious interior.

Even the cloud of the Shechinah continues this doctrine. The pillar of smoke by day and fire by night signifies divine nearness, but not in a crude physical sense. It is the sign that the sanctuary is a place where transcendent force overshadows ordered form. The ancient description of the cloud leveling obstacles, driving away serpents and scorpions, and guiding Israel through the wilderness turns the sanctuary into a moving center of orientation. Portable holiness means not that the sacred has become trivial, but that right order can travel through history, exile, and uncertainty.

The furnishings deepen the same pattern. The brazen altar at the entrance signifies that what must first be sacrificed is not some external beast but the lower, heavy, earth-bound part of the self. The bull and ram represent inner grossness, not merely animal victims. Hall openly criticizes literal sacrifice when detached from its inward meaning, because only what is surrendered from one's own nature can truly be offered. The Laver that follows signifies purification in body and soul alike. No one unclean in either domain can pass safely toward the divine presence.

Within the Holy Place, the great golden candlestick stands as one of the chapter's strongest images of illumined life. Its seven lamps, its branching form, and its ornamented stem make it at once cosmic and psychological. Opposite it stands the Table of Shewbread with its twelve loaves and ascending incense, suggesting ordered nourishment and the sanctification of life's sustaining powers. Between these and the final veil stands the Altar of Burnt Incense, which Hall reads as analogous to the human larynx. Here speech itself becomes offering. Words rise as incense when ordered rightly, and the entire sanctuary begins to resemble the interior constitution of a regenerated human being.

Beyond the veil is the Holy of Holies, containing only the Ark of the Covenant. The Ark, plated within and without, bearing the tablets of the Law and overshadowed by cherubim, is the concentrated center where memory, command, covenant, and presence converge. It is less a container than a throne of concealed nearness. There the Lord communicates, not because wood and gold can imprison divinity, but because a certain kind of order has been established and inward attention has been led to its utmost point of concentration.

Josephus' symbolic reading is then brought in explicitly to show that this was not a modern imposition. The three divisions of the sanctuary correspond to sea, land, and heaven. The twelve loaves signify the months or the zodiacal divisions of time. The candlestick points to planetary order and the veils to the four elements. The high priestly vestments similarly image the cosmos. In this reading, the Tabernacle is the world in reduced ceremonial form, and to move through it is to move through a condensed image of creation itself.

The chapter also assigns meaning to the metals. Gold signifies the spiritual principle that glorifies and surrounds human nature. Silver is linked with lunar purity and the regenerated human condition. Brass, especially in the outer courts, represents mixture, the human condition as compounded of noble and base elements. This gradation in metals parallels the path from outer mixture toward inward refinement. Hall also points out that the three divisions of the sanctuary later survive in Masonic symbolism and appear in other sacred architectures, including the chambers of the Great Pyramid.

The robes of the priests extend the same doctrine into living embodiment. The ordinary priest's white linen, sash, and cap already express purity and service, but the High Priest bears a fuller synthesis. Over the white robe comes the blue garment with alternating bells and pomegranates, then the Ephod woven of many colors and gold, then the breastplate with its twelve stones, then the crowned headdress with its flowerlike vessel at the top. None of this is mere pageantry. These vestments declare that the priest, if worthy, becomes a living cosmos, a bearer of correspondences between heaven and earth.

The jeweled shoulder stones and the breastplate oracles make this especially clear. The tribes of Israel are redistributed onto the body of the priest as living powers. The stones flash with divine answer because they signify awakened centers capable of receiving and radiating higher light. Hall connects them with zodiacal divisions, with the twelvefold deity, and with latent centers within human nature itself. The priest does not wear decoration to impress the crowd. He wears a diagram of ordered life and becomes, in ceremonial form, the mediator between distributed multiplicity and gathered unity.

The mysterious Urim and Thummim continue the same pattern. Their exact physical form may be uncertain, whether stones, lots, names, or talismanic signs, but their function is plain: sacred judgment requires a medium through which truth may be disclosed. Likewise the triple crown of the High Priest means rulership over several worlds at once, and the small golden flower above it signifies a receptive power at the crown of being, a vessel prepared to catch the descending waters of life. The true priest is therefore receptive before he is authoritative.

Yet the chapter's decisive turn comes at the end, where all ceremony is judged. Before entering the highest chamber, the High Priest lays aside visible magnificence. In the innermost presence he can rely only upon what he actually is. If a selfish thought arises, legend says he dies before the mercy seat, and the chain tied to his foot allows others to draw out his body without themselves entering the danger. The story is extreme, but its meaning is exact: sacred power cannot be compelled by ornament, office, or inherited status. The nearer man comes to the center, the fewer forms can shield him.

The enduring lesson of the Tabernacle is therefore not ritualism but construction of the inward temple. Sacrifice, cleansing, illumination, nourishment, right utterance, lawful memory, receptivity, and humility are stages by which life is made fit for divine nearness. The sanctuary matters because it teaches proportion. Its forms educate the soul into order. But the divine remains greater than every symbol, and in the end only the truth of being can stand before it.
