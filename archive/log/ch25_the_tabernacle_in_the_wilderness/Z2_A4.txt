This pass also reveals how strongly the chapter depends on the doctrine of correspondences. Astral, elemental, social, bodily, and ritual orders are all interwoven. The sanctuary's twelvefold and sevenfold structures align human life with calendrical and celestial rhythms. The fabrics and colors correspond to the elements. The metals correspond to degrees of constitution. What might appear as liturgical detail is actually a comprehensive attempt to bind every layer of existence into an intelligible pattern.

Because of this, the Tabernacle becomes a corrective to fragmented thinking. Religion, science, medicine, architecture, astronomy, psychology, and ethics are not separated in the chapter. Each becomes legible through the others. Hall's interpretation assumes a world in which truth is analogical and distributed rather than departmentalized.

The chapter thereby belongs with the book's strongest architectonic sections. It is not only about Hebrew worship but about the universal claim that true forms are generated by patterned relation among worlds. The sanctuary is a compressed diagram of that claim.
