Hall asks directly whether the divine knowledge of the ancient priesthoods truly perished with the outward destruction of their sanctuaries. His answer is no. The mysteries survived through persons rather than monuments, through emissaries who carried disciplined reason, occult science, and initiatory power into later ages under changing names and hostile conditions. The chapter is therefore a gallery of witnesses: not perfect historical biographies in the modern sense, but examples of how the perennial tradition continues to act through philosophers, martyrs, magicians, reformers, and hidden agents of culture.

He begins with a general principle. The excellence of a philosophical system is judged by the quality of the beings it produces. By that test, the mysteries stand vindicated, for they generated lives of such vision, discipline, and influence that later civilizations still live from their remnants. Yet those who bear an ideal beyond the understanding of the masses are almost always persecuted. Hall's roll call of Pythagoras, Hypatia, Savonarola, Bruno, Roger Bacon, and others prepares for the three figures he chooses to examine more closely as later emissaries of the hidden wisdom.

Hypatia stands first as the great woman philosopher of antiquity and one of the purest martyrs of pagan wisdom before triumphant fanaticism. Seated in the Alexandrian school, trained in Platonism and mathematics, consulted by magistrates and admired by students, she represented for Hall both the dignity of classical philosophy and the continuing power of mystery doctrine in an age of doctrinal conflict. Her murder by a Christian mob at the instigation or tolerated hatred of Cyril becomes one of the decisive symbols of the rupture between reasoned sacred philosophy and fanatical institutional religion. With her death, Hall suggests, an entire luminous mode of culture was violently eclipsed, though not extinguished forever.

The Comte di Cagliostro comes next as the most vilified modern adept. Hall does not deny the fog of legend surrounding him, but he treats the standard portrait of Cagliostro as a fabric of calumny magnified by the Inquisition and by later ignorance. He emphasizes instead the Comte's generosity, initiatory knowledge, symbolic reforms, and Egyptian Rite of Freemasonry. The episode in Paris, the influence on Masonic circles, his prophetic insight regarding the coming French Revolution, the Bastille inscription, and the eventual condemnation all show, for Hall, a man operating on behalf of a deeper current of esoteric culture while being publicly branded a charlatan. Cagliostro becomes the exemplary case of what happens when the world encounters a real adept but lacks the categories to recognize him.

St.-Germain then appears as the most dazzling of all modern emissaries: linguist, painter, chemist, musician, historian, diplomat, prophet, and master of occult arts. Hall delights in the breadth of his attainments, but the more important point is structural. St.-Germain moves through Europe under changing names, serving higher instructions, linked to Freemasonry, Rosicrucianism, and the secret brotherhood, and repeatedly escaping confinement within a single identity. His apparent agelessness, his mysterious appearances, his knowledge of bygone centuries, and his hidden retreat in the East all reinforce the sense that he belongs to a class of initiates who enter history when needed and withdraw when the work is done.

The chapter then shifts from Europe to America, where Hall finds suggestive traces of the same hidden guidance in the founding legends of the United States. The mysterious Professor who helped design the colonial flag, respected instantly by Washington and Franklin, and the unknown orator who appeared during the Declaration of Independence debate, electrified the assembly with the words that America had been given to be free, are treated as possible interventions by agents of the same secret tradition. Hall is less concerned to prove every detail than to assert a pattern: nations, like religions, may be seeded or redirected by emissaries carrying ideals far greater than the actors around them fully understand.

Thus the mysteries, in Hall's conception, are not dead archaeology. They remain a living though concealed institution whose representatives preserve philosophy, transmit symbols, protect culture, and intervene at moments of civilizational transition. Temples may fall, libraries may burn, reputations may be blackened, but the chain of initiates continues. The chapter's ultimate claim is that history itself becomes intelligible only when read with this hidden continuity in view.
