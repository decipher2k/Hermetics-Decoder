Hall approaches the Bacon-Shakspere controversy not as literary gossip but as a doorway into the survival of hidden wisdom. He declares at the outset that the real value of the problem lies not in displacing one dead author with another, but in recovering the doctrines that may have been concealed through dramatic and typographical art after the public mysteries had vanished. William Shakspere of Stratford becomes, in this perspective, less a sovereign genius than a possible mask. If so, the question is not merely who wrote the plays, but why an invisible school would choose concealment as the mode of transmission.

To weaken the traditional attribution, Hall rehearses the standard difficulties attached to the Stratford actor. The town in which Shakspere was raised offered no obvious training adequate to the range of learning displayed in the plays. His surviving signatures are few, uncertain, and unlike the spelling used on the title pages. No manuscripts of the dramas survive, no library is recorded in his possession, no books are mentioned in his will, and even his daughter remained illiterate. The worldly details of his life, including small financial lawsuits and commercial concerns, do not harmonize easily with the image of the universal dramatist. Hall accumulates these details not simply to attack Shakspere, but to make room for the possibility of deliberate substitution.

Against the Stratford figure he places Francis Bacon, whom he treats as courtier, lawyer, philosopher, reformer of science, editor of the Authorized Bible, and likely initiate of the Rosicrucian fraternity. Bacon possessed the education, languages, library, legal knowledge, continental experience, and philosophical range that the plays seem to require. More important still, Hall argues that the underlying ideas of the dramas are Rosicrucian in tone: Platonic, Qabbalistic, Pythagorean, Paracelsian, and initiatory. The Tempest, Macbeth, Hamlet, Cymbeline, and A Midsummer Night's Dream are not just skillful entertainments but vehicles of superphysical doctrine. Bacon therefore matters not merely as a candidate for authorship, but as the most plausible intermediary between an invisible brotherhood and public literature.

Hall then turns from biographical argument to the evidences of intentional concealment. He surveys emblematic headpieces, shaded and unshaded letters, title-page ornaments, watermarks, acrostics, catchwords, printer's devices, page misnumberings, numerical signatures, and other bibliographical anomalies scattered through seventeenth-century books. The light and dark A's, the hog or boar used as a play on Bacon's name, page 33 as Bacon's cipher number, deliberate-looking errors repeated across editions, acrostics spelling BACON, and cryptic signatures such as honorificabilitudinitatibus are presented as parts of a coordinated symbolic method. Books are treated as architectonic objects whose typography, ornaments, and apparent mistakes are as meaningful as their sentences.

This claim extends beyond the plays themselves. Hall points to Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy, the King James Bible, New Atlantis, Du Bartas, Alciati, Wither, Ralegh's History of the World, and the Great Folio as part of a wider Rosicrucian-Baconian field of encoded publication. The same signatures, ornaments, and devices recur across volumes, often in works linked to philosophy, scripture, empire, reform, or hidden learning. Such repetition persuades Hall that a secret fraternity was using print as a means of preserving and cross-indexing its doctrine. The outward author could be nominal; the real continuity lay in the symbolic system embedded across texts.

The Droeshout portrait and the Shakespeare folio receive special attention because they condense the alleged deception into one iconic object. Hall repeats the Baconian observation that the portrait looks like a constructed mask: oddly joined head and body, a strangely reversed coat, exaggerated lines, and a general air of caricature rather than likeness. The title page itself, he notes, contains a Rosicrucian numerical signature. If the face is a mask, then the book announces visually what the ciphers state textually: the visible author is not the whole truth. Literature here becomes ritual disguise.

Hall also connects the controversy directly with Freemasonry. He argues that modern Masonry emerged from the same secret societies that preserved ancient mystery wisdom under Christian and literary forms. Bacon knew, and perhaps helped shape, this process. One branch of the inheritance was organizational, becoming Freemasonry and transmitting its doctrine by symbol and rite. The other branch was bibliographic, embedding arcana in drama, philosophy, history, and scripture through ciphers and emblems. Solving the Bacon-Shakspere puzzle could therefore illuminate not only authorship but the lost rituals, origins, and transmission methods of the Masonic fraternity.

By the end of the chapter Bacon stands less as an isolated genius than as a focal point of a concealed order. Hall entertains even the possibility of a philosophic death, feigned burial, assumed names, and continued work under secrecy, because that would fit the pattern of initiatory self-effacement practiced by the custodians of the mysteries. The true labor was not to win fame, but to ensure that ancient wisdom survived religious and political hostility. On that reading, the plays and companion volumes are repositories of a deliberate, multi-layered revelation awaiting readers equipped with the proper key.

The chapter's final insistence is that decipherment must be pursued for the sake of wisdom, not vanity. The controversy matters because books may have become the new temples of the mysteries after the old sanctuaries fell. If so, typography, emblem, and cryptogram preserve a lineage running from the pagan hierophants through Rosicrucian brotherhoods into modern Freemasonry and literature. Hall therefore turns the authorship dispute into a much larger thesis: hidden fraternities can transmit doctrine through public culture while concealing both their identity and their treasure in plain sight.
