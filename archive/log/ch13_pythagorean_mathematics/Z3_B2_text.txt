This chapter is best read as an account of Pythagorean mathematics as exact symbolic intelligence. Hall presents number not as bare quantity, but as the formal medium through which hidden order becomes teachable, classifiable, and cosmologically meaningful.

The chapter begins from loss. The original oral key to the science largely vanished with the destruction of the school, leaving later readers with fragments, hints, and partial testimonies. Yet these remnants still preserve a clear direction: number is prior to the derivative sciences because relation is prior to shape, harmony, and motion.

This priority gives arithmetic its metaphysical dignity. Words return to original languages, larger values reduce to foundational digits, and the sciences themselves are led back to numerical structure. Surface multiplicity is intelligible only when traced to principle.

The monad stands as source and seed, while the decad gathers the whole field into completion. Between them unfolds a world of numerical differentiation: odd and even, composite and incomposite, perfect and deficient, stable and divisible, sufficient and excessive. These classes are not merely technical. They are ontological and ethical, expressing different modes of relation and measure.

The later table of the ten numbers turns arithmetic into symbolic cosmology. Each numeral names a power, a mode of emergence, a divine attribution, or a stage in the articulation of reality. Mythic naming and mathematical classification cooperate rather than compete. Sacred language preserves what formal relation discloses.

The chapter's lasting significance within the whole book is methodological as much as doctrinal. It trains the reader to think by proportion, center, polarity, sequence, and return. In doing so, it makes number one of the clearest bridges between esoteric wisdom and disciplined interpretation.
