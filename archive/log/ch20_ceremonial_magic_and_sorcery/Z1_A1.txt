Chapter 20 is structured as a warning disguised as a technical exposition. Hall explains ceremonial magic in enough detail to make its underlying logic intelligible, but the interpretive center of the chapter is ethical and metaphysical rather than operational. The problem is not merely ritual complexity. It is the attempt to compel power before mastering oneself.

Ceremonial magic is presented as a science of contact with unseen beings through symbols, vestments, names, circles, seals, timings, and invocations. Yet the chapter insists that this science bifurcates sharply according to motive and character. White and black magic are not separate arts so much as right and perverted uses of the same underlying power. The decisive issue is governance: who governs desire, force, symbol, and will.

Atlantis and corrupt priestcraft function here as archetypes of the institutionalization of occult abuse. The chapter therefore links private sorcery, false religion, and social domination as expressions of one deviation: the seizure of spiritual means for selfish power.
