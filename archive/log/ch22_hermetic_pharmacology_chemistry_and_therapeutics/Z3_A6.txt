The chapter reads healing as the restoration of harmony within a layered human constitution. Ancient medicine, Hall argues, belonged to the temple because philosophy, religion, and science were once understood as inseparable expressions of one wisdom. In that setting disease was traced not only to bodily disturbance but also to astral disorder, morbid thought, karmic compensation, celestial influence, etheric injury, impurity, or the breakdown of coordination between visible and invisible vehicles.

Paracelsus becomes the exemplary physician because he recovers this integral vision. Through concepts such as archæus, the universal life force, and mumia, the subtle vehicle that carries force, disease, or remedy, he explains why healing may require many different media. Herbs, talismans, sound, prayer, diet, sacred waters, astrology, and practical intervention are all valid insofar as they work upon the disturbed channel where life has been deflected.

The chapter's deeper teaching is that medicine becomes true when it reads causes before effects and assists Nature in re-establishing order. Healing is correspondential restoration.
