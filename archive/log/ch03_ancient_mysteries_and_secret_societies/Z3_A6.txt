Reformulation

The chapter's general law can now be stated plainly: inner doctrine survives by being dramatized. What is merely said can be forgotten or suppressed. What is staged, embodied, feared, desired, and passed through can lodge more deeply in persons and institutions.

Drama in the Mysteries is therefore not decorative. It is mnemonic, because strong symbolic experience is remembered. It is probative, because participation exposes character. And it is transmissive, because repeatable ceremony carries meaning across generations.

The chapter's examples reinforce this law from many directions. Druidic discipline dramatizes sacred order through rank and extended probation. Mithraic ceremony dramatizes struggle and mediation. Gnostic structures dramatize ascent through layers of concealment. Serapic systems dramatize descent into the underworld of bewilderment and reorientation. Northern forms dramatize wisdom through sacrifice and return. Eleusinian rites dramatize the soul's captivity and unveiling. Orphic and Bacchic currents dramatize fragmentation, hidden life, and restored wholeness.

Once this is seen, later inheritances become easier to read. Ritual orders may preserve ancient teachings precisely because they preserve ancient drama.

The danger, as always, is that drama can survive after doctrine weakens. But even reduced drama remains historically significant, because it may still encode fragments of an older science of transformation.

The chapter thus becomes an account of how dramatized doctrine outlives civilizational rupture.
