Hall opens with the medieval legend of the holy wood to show that the Christian cross was understood by symbolic thinkers as far more than a Roman instrument of execution. The tree begins in Eden, passes through Seth, Adam, Noah, Moses, David, Solomon, the Queen of Sheba, and Bethesda, and at last becomes the wood of the Crucifixion planted over Adam's skull. Hall treats this long genealogy not as naive folklore to be dismissed, but as an attempt to declare that the cross gathers into itself the whole history of fall, transmission, sacrifice, healing, and restoration. The cross is therefore linked to the tree of life, the rod of power, the temple pillar, the bridge between worlds, and the axis joining death to redemption.

From that legend Hall turns to a broader claim: the cross is not uniquely Christian in origin, but nearly universal in sacred symbolism. It appears among American Indians, in Japan and China, in Buddhist mandalas, in India, Egypt, Chaldea, Phoenicia, Assyria, and Greece. The initiates of Eleusis wore it, the Rosicrucians and alchemists read it as the sign of light, and many ancient peoples related it to nature worship, astronomy, and generation. Christianity, on this reading, inherited and reinterpreted an already ancient hieroglyph rather than inventing an unprecedented sign.

Hall then distinguishes the great forms of the symbol. The Tau cross, oldest and simplest, is linked to the hammer, the horns of sacred animals, the T-square, Mithraic initiation, Egyptian consecration, and the Pythagorean tetractys. The crux ansata, or ansate cross, joins the Tau to the circle or oval and becomes the sign of life, salvation, and passage through the mysteries. It is associated with Egypt, water symbolism, the Nile, and the granting of immortality. The familiar Roman and Greek crosses come later in the chapter as one family among many, not as the first or only legitimate form. By surveying these variants, Hall argues that the cross condenses several meanings at once: life and death, masculine and feminine, the four elements, the four directions, the fourfold constitution of man, and the mapping of heaven upon earth.

Astronomical and elemental readings deepen the symbol further. The cross may represent equinoxes and solstices, the intersection of celestial powers, or the division of the created world into four quarters. Its four arms correspond to earth, water, fire, and air, and to the four living creatures assigned by ancient esotericism to those elements. It can also be derived from the unfolded cube and thus related to the world of form. The material of the cross matters as well: gold, silver, wood, and base metal each alter the meaning. Because prayer itself often assumes the shape of outstretched arms, the cross also becomes a diagram of the human body and its fourfold expressive vehicles.

On this foundation Hall builds his central thesis about crucifixion. The death of the god-man on the cross is one of the oldest of the mystery dramas, long preceding Christianity in symbolic form. The divine life is stretched upon matter, the immortal principle is bound to the animal organism, and regeneration requires sacrifice, suffering, and conscious transcendence of the lower world. Initiatory systems enacted this through ritual crucifixion, symbolic entombment, and resurrection. The Christian story preserves the same law in a supreme and moving form, but it is part of a far older doctrine in which the Savior dies for mankind because spirit is continually offered up in the world of generation.

To substantiate the universality of the pattern, Hall invokes a whole procession of crucified or sacrificial saviors: Prometheus, Mithras, Christna, Quetzalcoatl, and others. Some, like Prometheus, are bound to a rock or tree for giving a divine gift to man. Some, like Mithras, are linked to annual rites of death and return. Christna's death by arrows beside or upon a tree, and Quetzalcoatl's cross in Central American symbolism, reveal for Hall that the crucifixion is not an isolated incident but a recurring dramatic statement about redemptive descent and ascent. Even where the details are obscured or allegorized, the same structure reappears.

The chapter closes by drawing the symbolism inward. The nails, the blood, the water, and the wound-centers of hands and feet all point to occult physiology and to the surrender of self-will. Quoting Böhmian and mystical interpretations, Hall presents the cross as terrestrial life, the crown of thorns as the soul's suffering in matter, and the crucified figure as the personal consciousness nailed down until the divine kingdom is established within. The true inscription above the head is read as In Nobis Regnat Jesus. Thus the final meaning of crucifixion is not merely historical commemoration but metaphysical realization: the lower self must be sacrificed so that the universal consciousness, imprisoned within personality, may be released.
