Hall introduces the fifteen diagrams from Georgius von Welling as one of the rare places where Rosicrucian and Qabbalistic doctrine is preserved in an explicitly visual form. Hargrave Jennings had reproduced only a few of them and left their meaning largely unexplained, but Hall insists that the entire series forms a coherent symbolic theology. The diagrams are not marginal curiosities. They condense into circles, triangles, cubes, letters, and planetary emblems a complete account of divine emanation, the ordering of the elements, the fall into material confusion, the constitution of the worlds, and the path of restoration. Von Welling does not fully interpret them because, in the Qabbalistic manner, the plates are intended to yield their deeper sense only to prolonged meditation.

The first tables establish the primordial structure of existence. The outermost fiery expanse, called Schamayim, is the divine ocean of spiritual fire and water in which all things subsist before they are separated into denser states. Within and beneath this field appear the seven spiritual intelligences, the subtle ethers, the region of earth and sea, and the more compressed subterranean zones in which celestial powers are condensed into corporeal forms. Hall emphasizes that the scheme is not a piece of naive astronomy but a metaphysical map showing how the superior and inferior worlds answer one another. The upper air has its lower counterpart, the sphere of the stars has its opposite in the subterranean atmosphere, and between higher and lower lies a reservoir receiving impressions from both. The elemental symbols that follow distinguish visible fire, air, water, and earth from their invisible and spiritual prototypes, and then gather them back into a single originating unity. What issues from the divine source must eventually return to it.

The second table develops the same doctrine through geometry and number. Spheres signify spirit because they rest upon a point and imply motion without angular fixity; cubes signify matter because they rest upon surfaces and denote weight, extension, and stable enclosure. Salt, treated as one of the primal substances, becomes the great emblem of manifested creation. Its cubical form yields six surfaces, twelve bodies, six pyramids, and the magical numbers seven, twenty-four, seventy-two, and a final vast arithmetic of cosmic cycles. Hall lingers over this numerology because it lets von Welling present creation as a measured architecture in which scriptural days, invisible church, angelic orders, and long periods of restoration are all contained in one figure. Lucifer's eventual release and reintegration are already hinted at numerically, suggesting that fall and redemption are built into the same total design.

From there the diagrams turn to lines, circles, and the first motions of manifestation. The dot becomes the sign of spirit, gold, sun, and germ. Moved forward it becomes a line; turned upon itself it becomes the circle, the most perfect figure and the generative pattern of all others. Figures of day and night, light and darkness, and elemental outflow show the breaking of the primal unity into the sixfold order of creation. Characters for air, water, fire, and salt display whether a given power descends from the superior fire or rises from the inferior realm, whether it reflects heavenly influence or carries the mark of inversion. The planetary and metallic characters then extend the same logic into the visible cosmos: gold and the sun share one perfect signature, silver and the moon another; copper, iron, tin, lead, mercury, antimony, sulphur, vitriol, and the salts are each assigned a glyph that states its occult constitution. Hall treats these not as arbitrary alchemical shorthand but as compressed statements about the inner tendencies of substances.

The later plates enlarge from isolated signs into full cosmological dramas. One chart divides the days of creation across concentric rings and interior globes, showing the emergence of light, darkness, the elemental divisions, the planetary ordering, and finally the human form on the sixth day. Another, famously interpreted as the symbolic tomb of Christian Rosencreutz, places the divine throne above, the seven spirits before it, the trinitarian flames in the center, and the descending outflow of divinity concentrating at the throne of Lucifer. From there the radiance passes through zodiacal orders and sacred planetary circles. Hall stresses that these diagrams preserve the Rosicrucian secret concerning Lucifer: he is not presented merely as a folkloric devil but as the focal point where divine light is seized, reflected, inverted, and contracted into the darkness of chaos. When he attempts to dominate power, the flow ceases, the once-spiritual field densifies, and a materialized cosmos emerges as the scene of consequence and eventual correction.

The charts that follow explore the worlds formed after this inversion. Figures of chaos, separation, and elemental stratification distinguish zones of goodness and depravity according to proximity to the infernal center. The elementals inhabiting air, water, earth, and subterranean regions are not morally uniform: some are luminous and wise, others deceptive, and others increasingly darkened as they descend toward the center of rebellion. Air is especially ambiguous because it carries beautiful spirits, demonic influences, human thoughts, stellar impressions, and the seed-patterns that shape organic forms. Hall uses these descriptions to show that the diagrams are intended to explain not only cosmology but psychic life. Man breathes and reflects a universe saturated with invisible impressions, and the intermediate worlds are therefore active participants in thought, generation, and illusion.

The remaining tables bring biblical history, judgment, microcosm, astrology, and regeneration into the same emblematic language. Triune sulphur, true vitriol, Copernican and biblical world-schemes, the last judgment, the synthesis of Old and New Testament planes, the New Jerusalem as cube, the creation of the solar system out of divine eternity, the division of the waters, Ain Soph, the divine triad, the world of Lucifer, the road to paradise, the pre-diluvian earth, and the final point surrounded by tribal and geometric signs all repeat one argument: visible history and invisible process mirror one another. The human being is a little world, planetary and zodiacal forces correspond to parts of the body, and the same mysteries that govern the rise of worlds govern the structure of scripture and the completion of the Great Work.

Hall's conclusion is that these plates guard doctrine in the most economical and least corruptible way. A geometry can hold an entire metaphysic at once: the emanation of the worlds, the balance of light and darkness, the relation of macrocosm and microcosm, the peril of false appropriation of divine power, the stratification of invisible beings, and the hope of reintegration. The diagrams therefore perform what Rosicrucianism always claimed for its symbols. They veil the doctrine from the careless eye, but to the disciplined student they reveal a total architecture of creation, fall, judgment, and return.
