Hall presents the North American Indian not as a primitive mind fumbling among superstitions, but as a natural symbolist whose consciousness remained close to the agencies of land, sky, season, and invisible life. The Indian world is populated with Manidos, lake peoples, cave races, winged beings, guardian spirits, and powers inhabiting every stratum of the universe. This is not mere fantasy for Hall. It expresses a metaphysical understanding in which visible and invisible realms interpenetrate, and in which the cosmos is divided into ordered regions above, below, and across the earth. Such a vision, he argues, is exactly the atmosphere in which mystery schools arise.

The sacred number four is one of the chapter's keys. The Indian universe is framed in four directions, four corners, four winds, and often four layers or great divisions, much as other ancient systems relied on the tetrad. This fourfold structure shapes ritual, cosmology, and sacred geography. The calumet ceremony becomes one of Hall's chief examples. When smoke is offered upward, downward, and sunward, the pipe becomes an altar, and the rite itself becomes a simple but exact declaration of man's relation to Great Spirit, Great Mother, and the life-giving heavens. The pipestone quarry, preserved as a place of peace between hostile tribes, further shows how sacred symbolism governed real institutions and conduct.

Hall also emphasizes that Indian religions distinguish subtle states of the dead, recognize guardians and totems, and preserve doctrines resembling reincarnation, transmigration, archetypal patterns, and elder group souls. Such ideas are not accidental curiosities. They indicate, in his view, a genuine science of soul and nature. The vision quest, by which a youth fasts and prays until his protecting spirit manifests, parallels initiatory election elsewhere in the world. The reverence for the serpent, especially the great serpent disgorging the egg of existence, reveals for Hall one more survival of universal mystery emblems in the Americas.

Hiawatha then appears as both historical reformer and symbolic initiate. In legend and memory he is culture-bearer, peacemaker, lawgiver, and later demigod. Hall notes that behind Longfellow's romantic rendering stands the more important image of a statesman who sought to end tribal strife by uniting nations in a league. The same pattern appears again: the true benefactor is misunderstood, attacked by sacerdotal hostility, vindicated only after departure, and elevated in memory into sacred narrative. Thus even political history among the red nations takes on mythic and initiatory form.

The chapter's deepest center is the Popol Vuh, which Hall regards as perhaps the clearest surviving American witness to a genuine initiatory ritual. In the descent into Xibalba, the deaths of the elder brothers, the miraculous conception of Xquiq through the head of Hunhun-ahpu, the trials of the hero twins, and their final triumph over the lords of death, he sees a complete mystery drama. Rivers of blood and mud, houses of darkness, spears, cold, tigers, fire, and bats, the decapitation of Hunahpu, the reduction to ashes, the transformation into fishes, the disguising as dancers, and the eventual destruction of the Xibalban princes all belong to a ritual sequence of death, testing, regeneration, and victory.

Hall draws a number of parallels from this material. The ordeals of Xibalba correspond to zodiacal or initiatory stages, the blowgun bridges and hidden roads suggest psychical channels and planes of descent, and the final resurrection of the hero twins reveals the same secret found in other mysteries: the spiritual consciousness must conquer the lower world by entering it, dying within it, and returning transfigured. He is especially concerned to show that this drama existed in the Americas independently and with philosophical depth equal to the better-known mysteries of the Old World.

The chapter closes with further evidence of initiatory institutions among American peoples, including Midewiwin records, birch-bark charts, healing rites, sacred lodges, and symbols comparable in method to Masonic trestleboards. Hall's final claim is direct: the Americas possessed their own custodianship of perennial wisdom. Their symbols, myths, ceremonies, and sacred books should be read not as ethnographic curiosities but as witnesses to profound metaphysical and initiatory traditions still insufficiently understood by the modern world.
