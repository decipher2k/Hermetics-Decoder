Hall ends the book by contrasting two kinds of conquest. Philip of Macedon sought for Alexander the best teacher and found Aristotle; Alexander went on to conquer lands, while Aristotle, through intellect, outlived him and ultimately mastered a far larger empire in the minds of men. This opening comparison establishes the whole conclusion: physical dominion fades, but rational and philosophical power endures. The true victory belongs not to the sword, but to understanding.

From there Hall turns to the present age with evident severity. Ancient cultures, whatever their limitations, understood that philosophy was not a pastime but a necessity, because without right knowing there can be no right living. Modern civilization, by contrast, pours its energy into industry, finance, mechanism, rivalry, and organized illusion. Men devote their lives to seizing temporary power in a world of transient things, yet neglect the one labor that might actually liberate them: the cultivation of the rational and spiritual self. In this inversion Hall sees the root of modern misery.

He attacks both materialistic science and materialized religion. Science, when reduced to classification of appearances and technical utility, binds man ever more tightly to the physical plane. Religion, when measured by buildings, property, and institutional prestige, loses beauty and inwardness. Even philosophy itself has often become dry, fragmented, and merely academic. Against all three distortions Hall sets the older understanding that philosophy is first a life and only secondarily a discourse. It is a disciplined transformation of the whole human being, not simply an arrangement of ideas.

Central to the conclusion is Hall's distinction between simple ignorance and complex ignorance. Simple ignorance is merely not knowing and can stimulate the search for truth. Complex ignorance is ignorance of ignorance, the dangerous state in which a creature mistakes partial knowledge for completeness and worships its own constructions. Modern civilization, in Hall's judgment, suffers chiefly from this second condition. It is dazzled by its own achievements, treats the earth as the center of significance, and mistakes commercial or political power for real greatness. In so doing it petrifies itself before the Medusa of greed.

The remedy is the restoration of the philosophic life. Right action, right feeling, and right thinking must support right knowing; body, emotions, and mind must be harmonized so that the rational faculty may rule. The old mysteries understood this and therefore trained their disciples over many years, not only through teaching but through disciplined living and initiation. Their aim was to accelerate the unfolding of the divine principle within man and to produce a mind capable of meeting any problem in the light of first principles. This, for Hall, is the real meaning of the Great Work and the true purpose of ancient wisdom traditions.

He then broadens the argument to culture as a whole. A nation, like a person, requires a philosophy of life if it is to attain genuine greatness. War, rivalry, and domination are signs of irrationality, and civilizations that build only outward power without inward order prepare their own destruction. Yet Hall does not end in despair. He insists that every new order worthy of the name must be founded on the preserved treasures of the old. The deepest records of human thought, the distilled wisdom of the sages, and the mysteries' methods of training reason must be carried forward if the next civilization is to be better than the last.

The book therefore closes as a call. Humanity's one hope is philosophy, not in the narrow academic sense, but as a way of being that unites reason, virtue, and spiritual aspiration. The Mysteries must rise again in spirit if not in their old forms, because only they reveal the world of causes behind the world of effects and teach the disciplined ascent from multiplicity toward unity. Hall's final position is clear: conscious immortality belongs to those who understand, and the future depends on whether enough men and women choose the philosophic life over the glittering futility of material ambition.
