Hall argues that no serious study of symbolism is complete without cryptography, because whenever sacred, philosophical, or politically dangerous truths must survive an age hostile to open speech, they are hidden in forms that reveal themselves only to trained perception. Military and diplomatic uses of ciphers are obvious, but literature, religion, and philosophy are equally saturated with them. The ancients first veiled truth with symbols, and later, when symbols themselves became too transparent, devised more intricate means of concealment. Nature, Hall says, already writes cryptically in forms; human sages merely imitate that universal habit by scattering meaning across letters, images, numbers, arrangement, and style.

He begins with the sacred languages and alphabets of the mysteries. Priests would not entrust divine verities to the same public tongue used for commerce and quarrel, so they developed restricted scripts and symbolic vocabularies. Yet even secret alphabets eventually betrayed the presence of hidden matter and could be broken by force or patience. This pushed the initiates toward a subtler goal: not merely obscuring a message, but concealing the fact that any message existed. Thus arose books whose obvious sense was innocent enough, while deeper teachings were embedded in typography, initials, pagination, bindings, watermarks, ornaments, or literary structure. Hall treats the circulation of such texts as a deliberate strategy by the illuminati to preserve wisdom without attracting persecution.

The chapter then surveys literal ciphers, beginning with Bacon's famous biliteral method. Two nearly identical alphabets, differing only in minute typographic details, allow each printed letter to function as either an a or a b; grouped in fives, these produce a concealed text. Hall explains the system at length because it shows how a message can be inserted into an otherwise ordinary document without changing its explicit wording. He also notes that biliteral methods may be layered with wheel ciphers and other substitutions, so that the first decipherment yields only a second enigma. In this way, apparent verbosity, awkward style, or typographic inconsistency may be the masking surface of technical intention.

From literal ciphers Hall moves to pictorial cryptograms. Egyptian, Hermetic, and alchemical images are never merely decorative. Letters may be hidden in arches, folds of garments, gestures, proportions, numbers of stones, patterns of shading, or deliberate distortions such as misdrawn limbs or reversed clothing. A pointing figure, a peculiar tool, or an anomalous posture may disclose the key of measurement. Under this heading Hall includes the emblem books, title pages, and diagrams beloved of Rosicrucians and alchemists, insisting that their imagery must be read as exact deposits of doctrine rather than as capricious ornament.

He then turns to what he calls the acroamatic cipher: parable, allegory, and myth. Here the text may be translated or recopied without destroying the hidden content, because the concealment lies in narrative structure itself. Scripture, Homer, Plato, Virgil, Apuleius, and Aesop all become examples. Hall stresses that such material admits several simultaneous readings, and that the moral interpretation accepted by ordinary religion is only one of many possible levels. The highest reading integrates the others and restores the philosophical or initiatory teaching beneath the story. Thus the gods, saviors, and creation myths of the world become not childish fictions but a coded alphabet of metaphysical truths.

Numerical, musical, arbitrary, and code ciphers extend the field further. Letters may be exchanged for numbers; names may be reduced to their numerical equivalents; missing or repeated page numbers may carry intention; every tenth, fiftieth, or otherwise counted word may disclose a message. Music may conceal language by note values or tonal equivalents. Arbitrary symbol systems may replace letters with invented signs, and modern codes may assign whole words or concepts to private signals. Hall does not merely catalogue these possibilities for curiosity's sake. He wants the reader to grasp how extensive the technical resources of concealment became, especially in the fifteenth through seventeenth centuries, when free inquiry often risked censure, imprisonment, or death.

Throughout the chapter Hall keeps returning to the same practical warning: apparent disorder may be method. Wordiness, strange initials, inconsistent printing, bizarre title pages, ornamental alphabets, acrostics, and alchemical jargon may all be devices of transmission. This is why philosophical, Rosicrucian, alchemical, and Masonic documents require a mode of reading different from ordinary prose. One must be prepared to count, compare, classify, measure, rotate, reorder, and reread.

The larger claim behind all of this is that cryptography is itself a school of mind. It sharpens discrimination, observation, patience, and symbolic intelligence. For Hall, the true student of wisdom must become alert not only to what is said but to how a text is built, spaced, ornamented, and apparently misshapen. In that sense cryptography is not an eccentric appendix to symbolic philosophy. It is one of its indispensable methods, because the guardians of hidden knowledge have repeatedly chosen to preserve their greatest treasures in forms visible to all yet legible only to the few.
