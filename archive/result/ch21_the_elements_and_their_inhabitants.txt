This chapter presents the visible world as the outer surface of a more intricate elemental ecology. Earth, water, fire, and air are each understood to possess not only a gross material body but also a subtler inward essence. Within these elemental ethers dwell the beings traditionally called Nature spirits: gnomes in earth, undines in water, salamanders in fire, and sylphs in air. These entities are neither immortal spirits nor ordinary corporeal creatures, but intermediate forms of life composed of a single elemental principle and therefore highly specialized within its domain.

Hall uses these elemental orders to interpret the differentiated processes of Nature. Gnomes govern solidity, minerals, roots, hidden growth, and structural support; undines preside over fluidity, moisture, beauty, vital essences, and the rhythms of water; salamanders embody heat, combustion, intensity, and the fiery agencies moving through blood and atmosphere; sylphs rule gases, winds, clouds, subtle communication, inspiration, and the volatile brilliance of thought and imagination. Because the human constitution contains relations to all four elemental fields, man can sometimes perceive or contact these kingdoms, though usually with confusion and only under exceptional conditions.

The chapter's deeper teaching is that subtle life must be carefully distinguished rather than indiscriminately feared or romanticized. Elementals are not the same as angels, demons, larvæ, incubi, succubi, or vampires. By sorting these orders of being, Hall restores lawful structure to the invisible world and presents Nature as animated by a distributed intelligence far more various than a purely mechanical account can explain. Symbolic personification here functions as a disciplined way of reading process, relation, and hidden agency within the cosmos.
